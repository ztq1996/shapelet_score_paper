\begin{abstract}
Image quality at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory is usually summarised by the PSF full width
at half maximum and ellipticity, both second-moment quantities dominated by the atmosphere.
Neither is sensitive to the asymmetric optical aberrations, chiefly coma and trefoil, so a visit can be taken in excellent seeing and still deliver a badly asymmetric PSF, which makes it unsuitable for many science analyses.  
We present the \emph{shapelet score} as a metric sensitive to exactly those aberrations
and blind to the atmosphere by construction. Decomposing each PSF into polar shapelets, we
show on simulated point-symmetric atmospheric PSFs that the coefficients with odd spin $m$
vanish, while the even-$m$ coefficients absorb all
the atmospheric variation. We define the \emph{shapelet score} as the odd-$m$ fraction of
shapelet power.
We measure the shapelet score on for $1{,}949{,}189$ raft-stacked PSFs from $94{,}422$ LSSTCam visits taken between 2025 April and 2026 July. 
We show visual evidence that the shapelet score strongly correlates with the irregularity of the PSF. We demonstrate that the shapelet score can be used as a nightly monitor metric for image quality. 
We propose the shapelet score as an image-quality metric complementary to PSF size and to the existing AOS-derived metrics.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{intro:0}
The NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory will carry out the Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(LSST), imaging the southern sky in six bands over ten years with the 3.2-gigapixel LSST
Camera (LSSTCam) on the 8.4\,m Simonyi Survey Telescope \cite{Ivezic2019}. Much of the
science LSST will deliver rests on the quality of the images it takes. Photometry,
astrometry and galaxy shape measurement are all carried out against a model of the point
spread function (PSF) \cite{Bosch2018}, which for a ground-based telescope is a convolution
of atmospheric PSF, the optical system, dome seeing and the detector. A more compact PSF
directly increases the scientific return of a given exposure: the number density of
galaxies whose shapes can be measured is set in large part by their size relative to the
PSF \cite{Chang2013}, and a smaller PSF separates close pairs that would otherwise be
blended into a single detection, which is a known source of bias in shear measurement
\cite{Dawson2016, Nourbakhsh2022}. Conversely, a PSF that is poorly modelled leaks directly
into galaxy shape measurements and hence into cosmological parameter constraints
\cite{HirataSeljak2003, Mandelbaum2018, Jarvis2021, Zhang2023}, at a level for which the
survey's precision targets leave little room \cite{DESC_SRD}. Monitoring the delivered PSF
night by night and across the focal plane is therefore both an operational requirement and
a prerequisite for the survey's precision-cosmology goals. That monitoring has to track not
only how large and how elliptical the delivered PSF is, but also how irregular and
asymmetric it is.

In practice, image quality is often summarised by second-moment quantities: the full width
at half maximum (FWHM) and the two components of the PSF ellipticity. These are cheap to
compute and capture the dominant behaviour of the PSF, but for our purpose they have two
shortcomings. The first is that they are dominated by the atmosphere. Ground-layer seeing
sets the FWHM and contributes most of the ellipticity, so a visit taken through good
seeing will report excellent second-moment image quality even when the optical system is
misaligned. The failure modes that the Rubin active optics system (AOS) attempts to correct
are precisely those to which second moments are least sensitive: coma, trefoil, and
higher-order asymmetric aberrations from mirror figure error, hexapod misalignment, or
incomplete AOS convergence \cite{Xin2015}. A compact, nearly round PSF can still be badly
aberrated, and an aberration of this kind can sit undetected behind a reassuring FWHM.

The second shortcoming is that an irregular PSF degrades measurements in its own right,
whatever its size. Part of the reason is that an asymmetric PSF is harder to model. PSF
models are interpolated across the focal plane from a finite number of stars using a basis
of limited flexibility \cite{Jarvis2021}, so the higher-order, asymmetric structure of the
profile is typically the part least well captured, and whatever the model misses is left
behind as a residual. Those residuals then propagate into the measurements. Standard
centroiding algorithms return a biased position in the presence of an asymmetric PSF
\cite{Pier2003}, and more generally inaccuracies in the PSF model propagate directly into
measured positions, with astrometry more sensitive to them than photometry is
\cite{AndersonKing2000}. For weak lensing, the moments of the PSF beyond second order
propagate into the inferred shear and contribute a systematic that the second-moment
description does not capture \cite{Zhang2023}. A small but irregular PSF therefore carries
a cost of its own, over and above what it indicates about the state of the optics.

The observatory already measures the wavefront directly, through annular Zernike
coefficients estimated by curvature sensing at the four corner wavefront sensors
\cite{Xin2015}, and these are a powerful and well-established diagnostic of the optical
state. They are, however, measured at only four positions at the edge of the focal plane,
while a metric computed from the science images themselves samples the full focal plane
and describes the PSF that the science measurements actually see. We regard the two as
complementary: an independent handle on image quality, derived from a different instrument
and a different analysis path, is valuable in its own right.

This motivates a metric built from the science images, sensitive to exactly the asymmetric
optical modes that second moments miss, and insensitive to the atmosphere by construction.
We decompose each PSF into a polar shapelet basis, the Gauss-Laguerre eigenfunctions of
the two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator
\cite{Refregier2003, MasseyRefregier2005, BernsteinJarvis2002}, and propose the
\emph{shapelet score}, defined as the fraction of the total shapelet power carried by the
modes with odd spin $m$. Those modes vanish identically for any point-symmetric
profile, so the score responds only to genuine asymmetry in the image. We use a simulated
population of atmospheric PSFs, spanning a realistic range of size, ellipticity and radial
profile shape, to show that the score is decoupled from the atmospheric component of the
PSF: across the simulated population the odd-$m$ coefficients stay at the level of
numerical round-off, while the even-$m$ coefficients absorb all of the atmospheric
variation.

We measure the shapelet score for $1{,}949{,}189$ raft-stacked PSFs drawn from $94{,}422$
LSSTCam visits spanning April 2025 to July 2026, covering both the Data Preview 2 (DP2)
processing \cite{DP2} and subsequent nightly validation. Inspecting stacked PSF images
across the range of the score, we demonstrate that it is a good metric for PSF
irregularity: low-scoring stamps are round and compact, while high-scoring stamps show the
displaced cores and one-sided flares characteristic of coma and trefoil. We also compute a
complementary \emph{moment score} from the spin-1 (coma) and spin-3 (trefoil) combinations
of the third-order adaptive moments of the same stars, built from entirely different
machinery, and find the two scores in close agreement, at a Spearman rank correlation of
$\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$. We then study how the shapelet score correlates with a range of
other quantities: the delivered PSF size, the AOS estimates of image quality, and the
wavefront Zernike coefficients measured at the corner sensors. The score
is computed by the LSST Science Pipelines and published per detector and visit in the
\texttt{DetectorVisitProcessingSummary} table of the Rubin science data model schemas
\cite{SDMSchemas}, so it is available directly as a record of delivered image quality. We
propose the shapelet score as a complement to the PSF size and AOS-derived metrics already
in use.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{data:0} describes the LSSTCam data and the
auxiliary catalogs. Section~\ref{method:0} sets out the shapelet formalism, presents the
atmospheric simulation that motivates the mode selection, and defines both scores.
Section~\ref{results:0} presents the measured distributions, validates the scores visually
and against the higher-order moments, and demonstrates their behaviour as a function of
observing conditions and of time. We conclude in Section~\ref{conclusion:0}.

\subsection{PSF star stamps}
\label{data:stamps}

We build our PSF sample from cutouts of the stars the pipeline used to constrain the PSF
model: the \texttt{refit\_psf\_star} catalog for DP2, and the \texttt{single\_visit\_star}
catalog restricted to \texttt{calib\_psf\_used = True} for post-DP2. Stamps are cut
directly from the \texttt{preliminary\_visit\_image} pixels at the catalog centroids. We
restrict the analysis to the 21 science rafts.

For each visit and each science raft we select one detector at random, draw up to 100
stars from it, cut a $51 \times 51$ pixel stamp around each, normalise each to unit flux,
and average them to increase the signal-to-noise ratio of the shapelet decomposition.
Stars whose stamps would cross a detector edge are rejected. This yields one stacked PSF
image per raft per visit.

\subsection{Higher-order moment catalog}
\label{data:moments}

For the moment score of Section~\ref{method:moment_score} we use the adaptive-moment
measurements produced by the LSST Science Pipelines for the same PSF stars. Third-order
moments come from the \texttt{ext\_shapeHSM\_HigherOrderMomentsSource} plugin, which
measures the normalised moments $M_{pq}$ in the coordinate frame standardized by the HSM
adaptive second moments \cite{HirataSeljak2003, BernsteinJarvis2002}, making them
dimensionless and second-moment-independent \cite{Zhang2023}. From the per-star measurements we form, for each visit, the
median of every moment over the stars in each science raft.

\subsection{Wavefront and observing-condition metadata}
\label{data:zernike}

We take three classes of auxiliary metadata from the Consolidated Database (consDB), matched
to our visits by \texttt{visit\_id}.

\textbf{Wavefront Zernikes.} The Rubin AOS estimates the wavefront by curvature sensing on
the four corner wavefront sensors \cite{Xin2015}. From the \texttt{ccdvisit1\_quicklook}
table we retrieve the annular Zernike coefficients $z_4$ through $z_{11}$ at each corner in
the Noll ordering \cite{Noll1976}, covering defocus, astigmatism, coma, trefoil and
spherical, and form the peak-to-peak spread $\max_j z_i^{(j)} - \min_j z_i^{(j)}$ over the
four corner positions $j$ as a proxy for the aberration gradient across the field.

\textbf{Delivered image quality.} We use \texttt{aos\_fwhm}, the AOS estimate of the
delivered FWHM, and \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}, the FWHM contribution inferred from the
donut images on the wavefront sensors. Both are independent of our shapelet measurement and
provide the comparison baseline in Section~\ref{results:fwhm}.

\section{THE SHAPELET SCORE}
\label{method:0}

In this section we define the shapelet score. In Section~\ref{method:shapelet} we set out
the shapelet decomposition of the PSF. In Section~\ref{method:atm} we present a simple
atmospheric PSF simulation and show that the odd-$m$ shapelet coefficients are insensitive
to the atmosphere, which motivates the definition of the score in
Section~\ref{method:shapelet_score}. In Section~\ref{method:moment_score} we define a
complementary moment score that goes with the shapelet score, built from the third-order
adaptive moments of the same stars. In Section~\ref{method:tiers} we reduce the score to
four quality tiers, and validate those tiers directly against example PSF stamps.

\subsection{Shapelet decomposition of the PSF}
\label{method:shapelet}

Shapelets are the eigenfunctions of the two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator, a
complete orthonormal basis in which a localised image can be expanded to a chosen maximum
order \cite{Refregier2003, BernsteinJarvis2002}. We use the polar (Gauss--Laguerre) form
\cite{MasseyRefregier2005}, whose basis functions carry a definite azimuthal dependence
$e^{im\theta}$ and therefore separate the image into components of explicit rotational
symmetry. 
We write the surface brightness of a PSF as
\begin{equation}
I(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{pq} b_{pq}\,
               \psi_{pq}\!\left(\frac{r}{\sigma}, \theta\right),
\label{eq:shapelet}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is an overall scale length, $b_{pq}$ are the shapelet coefficients, and the
basis functions are
\begin{equation}
\psi_{pq}(r, \theta) = \frac{(-1)^q}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sqrt{\frac{q!}{p!}}\;
                       r^{m} e^{im\theta} e^{-r^2/2} L_q^{(m)}(r^2),
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
with $L_q^{(m)}$ the generalised Laguerre polynomials. The indices $p$ and $q$ count quanta
of positive and negative angular momentum; we label modes instead by the order $n = p + q$
and the spin $m = p - q$, so that $n$ and $m$ always have the same parity. We call $m$ the
spin because it is the spin weight of the mode under rotation: rotating the image by an
angle $\phi$ multiplies $b_{nm}$ by $e^{i m \phi}$. This is the same sense in which the
third-moment combinations of Section~\ref{method:moment_score} are spin-1 and spin-3.
Requiring $I(r,\theta)$ to be real imposes $b_{pq} = b_{qp}^{*}$, so each $m > 0$ mode
contributes one real and one imaginary coefficient and each $m = 0$ mode a single real one.

We truncate at $n \le 6$, which gives the $28$ coefficients shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:basis}. We fit each stacked PSF stamp using \textsc{GalSim}
\cite{Rowe2015}: we measure the adaptive moments with \texttt{FindAdaptiveMom}, which
implements the HSM algorithm \cite{HirataSeljak2003}, adopt the resulting
$\sigma_{\rm HSM}$ (in arcseconds) as the shapelet scale $\sigma$, and solve the linear
least-squares problem for $b_{pq}$ with \texttt{Shapelet.fit} in surface-brightness
normalisation. Setting the scale from the adaptive moments is what makes the decomposition
approximately scale-free. Throughout, we quote FWHM as $2.3548\,\sigma_{\rm HSM}$.

\subsection{Atmosphere-insensitive shapelet modes}
\label{method:atm}

In this section, we use a simple image simulation to demonstrate which shapelet
coefficients are insensitive to the atmospheric PSF.

We generate $1000$ realisations of an atmospheric PSF, each the sum of two Moffat profiles
with independently drawn parameters: FWHM uniform on $[0.5, 1.2]\arcsec$, Moffat $\beta$
uniform on $[2.5, 4.5]$, and shear components $g_1, g_2$ drawn from a normal distribution of
width $0.03$ and clipped at $|g| < 0.3$. We use a two-component profile to mimic the more
realistic case of a changing atmospheric PSF, which can vary in size, shape and radial
profile within a single exposure. Crucially, each component is centred at the origin, so
every realisation is point-symmetric: the population contains atmospheric variation and, by
construction, no optical asymmetry whatsoever. We fit each with the same procedure used for
the data, recovering all $1000$ fits with measured FWHM spanning $0.56$--$1.32\arcsec$.

Figure~\ref{fig:atm} shows the sensitivity of each shapelet coefficient to the variable
atmospheric PSF, as the standard deviation of the coefficient across realisations. The
even-$m$ coefficients vary substantially, whereas every odd-$m$ coefficient has
$\sigma \le 1.4 \times 10^{-16}$, zero at the level of double-precision round-off. The
odd-$m$ coefficients are therefore effectively insensitive to atmospheric variation. This
follows from symmetry alone: any point-symmetric PSF has no odd-$m$ power at all, whether a
Gaussian, a Moffat, a Kolmogorov profile, an elliptical version of any of these, or the PSF
of a perfectly aligned telescope.

\subsection{The shapelet score}
\label{method:shapelet_score}

Motivated by Section~\ref{method:atm}, we define the shapelet score as the fraction of the
total shapelet power carried by the odd-$m$ modes:

\begin{equation}
\sshape = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n \geq 3,\; m\ \mathrm{odd}} |b_{nm}|^2}
               {\displaystyle\sum_{nm} |b_{nm}|^2} .
\label{eq:shapelet_score}
\end{equation}

At $n \le 6$ the numerator runs over five modes, $b_{33}$, $b_{31}$, $b_{55}$, $b_{53}$ and
$b_{51}$, or ten of the 28 real coefficients once real and imaginary parts are counted
separately. The score is dimensionless, bounded in $[0, 1]$, and rotationally invariant.
Normalising by the total power makes it independent of the stamp flux
normalisation, and, because $\sigma$ is set by the adaptive moments, largely independent of
PSF size. We test this last claim directly in Section~\ref{results:fwhm}.

Two exclusions deserve comment. First, we exclude the $n = 1$ modes, which are odd in $m$
but describe a displacement of the profile rather than a distortion of it: a stamp whose
centroid is offset from the fitted centre acquires $n = 1$ power without being asymmetric,
so including them would make the score sensitive to stamp registration. This matters in
practice, since in the $i$-band data the $n = 1$ modes carry a median fractional power of
$1.8 \times 10^{-4}$, roughly a quarter of the typical score. The LSST Science Pipelines
publish both choices for every detector-visit in the
\texttt{DetectorVisitProcessingSummary} table of the Rubin science data model schemas
\cite{SDMSchemas}: \texttt{shapeletsOnlyIqScore} is built from the non-atmospheric
coefficients alone, while \texttt{shapeletsIqScore} additionally includes power from the
median offset between the centroids used in the decomposition and those of the centroid
slot. The score we define here is the analogue of the former. Second, we truncate at
$n \le 6$; higher orders would add further odd-$m$ modes, but at fixed stamp size and noise
level they are increasingly poorly constrained, and we find $n \le 6$ already captures the
asymmetries visible by eye (Section~\ref{method:tiers}).

\subsection{The moment score}
\label{method:moment_score}

We construct a second, independent metric from the third-order adaptive moments of
Section~\ref{data:moments}, both as a cross-check and because moments are already computed
by the LSST Science Pipelines \cite{Pipelines2026} for every star. Following
Ref.~\citenum{Zhang2023}, we form the spin-1 and spin-3 combinations of the normalised
third moments $M_{pq}$,

\begin{align}
c_{11} &= M_{30} + M_{12},  &  c_{12} &= M_{21} + M_{03}, \label{eq:coma}\\
c_{31} &= M_{30} - 3 M_{12}, &  c_{32} &= 3 M_{21} - M_{03}, \label{eq:trefoil}
\end{align}

where $(c_{11}, c_{12})$ transform as spin-1 under rotation and describe the coma-like
asymmetry of the PSF, and $(c_{31}, c_{32})$ transform as spin-3 and describe the
trefoil-like asymmetry. The moment score is the weighted sum of their squared moduli,

\begin{equation}
\smom = 3\left(c_{11}^2 + c_{12}^2\right) + \left(c_{31}^2 + c_{32}^2\right),
\label{eq:moment_score}
\end{equation}

which, like the shapelet score, is dimensionless and rotationally invariant. The factor of
three up-weights the coma term; we adopt it for consistency with the metric already in use
for observatory monitoring, and note that the choice affects the normalisation of $\smom$
but not strongly its rank ordering, since coma and trefoil are themselves correlated.

We note that the two scores capture the same physical information, the asymmetric odd-$m$
content of the PSF, but one reaches it by a least-squares basis expansion of the image and
the other by weighted pixel moments. We quantify their agreement in
Section~\ref{results:score_comparison}.

\subsection{Score tiers and visual validation}
\label{method:tiers}

For operational use it is convenient to reduce the score to a small number of quality
classes. We adopt four tiers: \textbf{LOW} ($\sshape < 0.002$), clean, symmetric PSFs;
\textbf{MEDIUM} ($0.002 \le \sshape < 0.005$), mild asymmetry; \textbf{HIGH}
($0.005 \le \sshape < 0.02$), clearly visible coma or trefoil; and \textbf{VERY HIGH}
($\sshape \ge 0.02$), strongly aberrated PSFs. These are round numbers chosen to sit near
the $80$th, $95$th and $99$th percentiles of the global distribution, which we justify
against the measured percentiles in Section~\ref{results:distributions}.

We visually check whether the tier system correctly reflects the quality of the PSF by eye.
Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} shows three $i$-band raft-stacked PSFs from each tier, each next to
its shapelet reconstruction. For LOW and VERY HIGH we show the extremes of the distribution,
and for the two intermediate tiers PSFs near the geometric midpoint, so that the figure
spans the full visual range of the metric.

The progression is clear by eye. LOW-tier stamps are round and compact; MEDIUM-tier stamps
show mild elongation; HIGH-tier stamps display an unmistakable comatic profile, a displaced,
asymmetric core with a one-sided flare; and VERY HIGH-tier stamps are strongly aberrated,
one showing the double-lobed morphology characteristic of a large misalignment term. The
shapelet model reconstructs the PSF correctly in every tier, reproducing the asymmetric
features as well as the core, and the visibly aberrated PSFs carry a clearly higher score.

The annotated moment scores track the tiers independently, with ranges disjoint across all
four: $\smom$ spans $2.7 \times 10^{-6}$ to $3.8 \times 10^{-5}$ for the LOW examples,
$0.0076$--$0.0154$ for MEDIUM, $0.0376$--$0.0525$ for HIGH and $0.095$--$0.236$ for VERY
HIGH. Since the moment score shares no machinery with the shapelet fit, this ordering is a
non-trivial consistency check. The annotated FWHM values, by contrast, are interleaved
across tiers. The LOW examples have the \emph{largest} FWHM of any row, at
$1.17$--$1.84\arcsec$, against $0.92$--$1.36\arcsec$ for HIGH, anticipating the result of
Section~\ref{results:fwhm} that PSF size and PSF asymmetry are close to independent.

\label{results:0}

\subsection{Score distributions}
\label{results:distributions}

Figure~\ref{fig:dist} shows the distribution of the shapelet score in each band, for both
epochs, and Table~\ref{tab:percentiles} lists the corresponding percentiles. The
distribution is broad and strongly right-skewed: it peaks near $S_{\rm shapelet} \approx
10^{-4}$ and extends over more than two orders of magnitude, with a long tail above
$10^{-2}$.

The distributions are similar in all six bands but shift systematically to higher scores
with wavelength: in DP2 the median rises from $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$ in $u$ to $4.9 \times
10^{-4}$ in $y$. Two plausible contributions, which we do not attempt to separate here, are
that the redder bands see better seeing, so a fixed optical aberration represents a larger
fraction of the total PSF power, and that the $u$ band has the fewest and faintest stars per
stamp, so its scores are the most affected by noise. Comparing epochs, post-DP2 has modestly
higher medians in $u$, $i$, $z$ and $y$, the $i$-band median rising from
$4.0 \times 10^{-4}$ to $4.9 \times 10^{-4}$, but a lighter upper tail: its P99 is lower
than in DP2 in all six bands, and its P95 in all but $y$. We caution against reading the
shift in the median as a simple degradation, since post-DP2 covers a period of active work on
the telescope optics, and Section~\ref{results:monitoring} shows that the elevated scores are
concentrated in 2026 January to March rather than spread uniformly over the epoch.

The measured distributions bear out the tier boundaries of Section~\ref{method:tiers}. The
last four columns of Table~\ref{tab:percentiles} give the resulting tier occupancy of each

\subsection{Dependence on PSF size}
\label{results:fwhm}

In this section we study a key property of the shapelet score, its independence from the
PSF size. Three size metrics are available: the FWHM measured from the stacked PSF stamp
itself, and two per-visit estimates from the active-optics system, the AOS FWHM
(\texttt{aos\_fwhm}) and the donut-blur FWHM (\texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}). In
Figure~\ref{fig:fwhm} we show the scatter of the shapelet score against all three, for both
epochs, with all six bands pooled. We note that the two AOS quantities are per visit, so
every raft of a visit carries the same value, whereas the measured FWHM is that stamp's
own.

We find no appreciable correlation between the score and the measured PSF FWHM in either
epoch, with a Spearman coefficient of $\rho_{\rm s} = -0.043$ in DP2 and $-0.232$ in
post-DP2; no individual band exceeds $|\rho_{\rm s}| = 0.28$.

The more important point is the vertical extent of the distribution. Averaged over bins
between $0.6\arcsec$ and $1.5\arcsec$, the ratio of the $95$th to the $5$th percentile at
fixed FWHM is $76$ in DP2 and $102$ in post-DP2, whereas the running median, the orange
line, varies by only a factor of $1.9$ and $2.7$ over the same range. The scatter at fixed
seeing is therefore one to two orders of magnitude larger than any trend with seeing:
knowing the FWHM of a visit tells one almost nothing about whether its PSF is aberrated,
and PSFs in the HIGH and VERY HIGH tiers are found at all seeing values, including the
best.

We do note a real, if weak, effect in the post-DP2 panel, where the median score falls by a
factor of $2.6$ between $0.8\arcsec$ and $1.5\arcsec$. This is not a dilution of the score
by atmospheric power entering its denominator. The denominator is almost entirely the
lowest-order term: $b_{00}^2$ carries a median $99.3\%$ of the total shapelet power in both
epochs, because the PSF is close to a Gaussian of the fitted scale. Since $b_{00}$ is
proportional to the flux, and our stamps are normalised to unit flux
(Section~\ref{data:stamps}), dividing by the total power amounts to little more than a flux
normalisation, and leaves no appreciable atmospheric term for a larger PSF to inflate.
Consistent with this, the un-normalised odd-$m$ power falls over the same FWHM range by a
factor of $0.41$ in post-DP2, against $0.38$ for the score itself. Any suppression of the
score in poor seeing therefore acts on the odd-$m$ power itself, through the convolution of
the optical asymmetry with a broader atmospheric kernel, and not through the normalisation.

Part of the trend is also a confound between seeing and the state of the optics. Post-DP2
covers a period of active work on the telescope, and the intervals with the most aberrated
PSFs happened also to be intervals of good seeing (Section~\ref{results:monitoring}).
Stratifying by night and by band removes both the slow drift of the optics and the tendency
of the redder bands to have better seeing and higher scores at the same time. Within those
strata the correlation reverses sign: over (night, band) strata containing at least $200$
PSFs, the median Spearman coefficient is $+0.253$ in DP2, positive in $81\%$ of strata, and
$+0.045$ in post-DP2, positive in $57\%$.

The two AOS metrics behave very differently from each other, and understanding why is
instructive. The score correlates \emph{positively} with \texttt{aos\_fwhm}
($\rho_{\rm s} = +0.376$ in DP2 and $+0.345$ in post-DP2) but \emph{negatively} with
\texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} ($-0.292$ and $-0.496$). The negative sign is at first sight
puzzling, since a larger donut blur should indicate a larger wavefront error and therefore
a more aberrated PSF. Part of the explanation is that \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} is close
to a proxy for the delivered image size, tracking the measured PSF FWHM at
$\rho_{\rm s} = +0.797$ in DP2 and $+0.761$ in post-DP2, whereas \texttt{aos\_fwhm} tracks
it much more weakly ($+0.353$ and $+0.201$).

That proxy argument does not account for the whole effect. Controlling for the measured PSF
FWHM by partial rank correlation leaves the \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} trend essentially
unchanged, at $-0.401$ in DP2 and $-0.476$ in post-DP2, and strengthens the
\texttt{aos\_fwhm} trend slightly, to $+0.424$ and $+0.412$. We note that this test is not
decisive, because the measured PSF FWHM contains the optical contribution as well as the
atmospheric one, so holding it fixed does not hold the atmosphere alone fixed. We therefore
report the negative correlation with \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} as an unexplained feature
of these data rather than as established dilution. We also caution that we have taken both
consDB quantities at face value without tracing their definitions through the AOS pipeline,
so this discussion rests on the measured correlations alone.

\subsection{Link to optical aberration}
\label{results:optics}

The strongest test of whether the shapelet score measures optical aberration is to compare
it against an independent optical measurement. The four corner wavefront sensors provide
exactly that, estimating $z_4$--$z_{11}$ from curvature sensing and using neither the
science pixels nor any part of our analysis.

Figure~\ref{fig:zernike} shows how the visit-median score responds to each of these eight
field spreads, and the ordering follows the prediction of the mode selection remarkably
closely. Between the lowest and the highest quintile of field spread, the median shapelet
score rises by a factor of $2.35$ for $z_8$ and $2.05$ for $z_7$, by $1.53$ and $1.40$ for
$z_{10}$ and $z_9$, and by only $1.10$ for $z_5$. The same ordering appears in the rank
correlations in the left panel, where the two coma modes are the only ones to reach
$\rho_{\rm s} \simeq +0.17$ and $+0.20$.

The two coma modes lead this ordering and the trefoil modes follow, which is the direction
we would expect if the score responds to asymmetric aberration. We do not read more into
the ordering than that. The separation between odd-$m$ and even-$m$ modes is not clean, the
astigmatism mode $z_6$ giving a ratio of $1.42$, comparable to the trefoil modes, and we
have no explanation for the difference between $z_5$ and $z_6$. The corner sensors also
sample the wavefront at only four field positions, all outside the science rafts on which
the score is measured, so the four-point spread is a crude proxy for the true field
dependence and we expect it to dilute the correlations substantially.

The field spread is not the only way to use the corner measurements. Taking instead the
\emph{magnitude} of each aberration, averaged over the four corner sensors, we find that
defocus is by far the strongest predictor of the score, at
$\rho_{\rm s} = +0.357$ for $|z_4|$ against $+0.188$ for the trefoil magnitude, $+0.152$ for
astigmatism and $+0.062$ for coma. This correlation is robust in a way the others are not.
Computed within individual nights, which removes the slow drift of the optical state, it is
unchanged at $+0.352$ and positive on $88\%$ of the $84$ nights with at least $100$ visits.
It is also not a proxy for the other aberrations or for PSF size: the partial rank
correlation is $+0.353$ holding the coma magnitude fixed, $+0.343$ holding trefoil fixed and
$+0.406$ holding the measured FWHM fixed, whereas the coma correlation falls to $-0.008$
once $|z_4|$ is held fixed.

Defocus and the asymmetric aberrations also interact, and the interaction suggests that
defocus is what makes the others visible. Splitting the sample into quartiles of $|z_4|$,
the rank correlation between the score and the coma magnitude rises monotonically from
$-0.078$ in the sharpest quartile, of median $|z_4| = 0.09\,\mu$m, to $+0.070$ in the most
defocused, of median $0.64\,\mu$m. The mirror statistic behaves the same way: the
correlation between the score and $|z_4|$ rises from $+0.222$ in the quartile with the least
coma to $+0.463$ in the quartile with the most. The median score itself tells the same
story. At the sharpest focus it is flat against coma, at $2.9$, $2.4$, $2.1$ and
$2.3 \times 10^{-4}$ across coma quartiles, whereas at the most defocused it rises
monotonically, at $7.6$, $8.5$, $8.8$ and $9.6 \times 10^{-4}$. We suspect the mechanism is
that a defocused PSF is partly an image of the pupil, so that a pupil asymmetry which is
invisible at best focus prints directly onto the stamp, but we have not tested this.

\subsection{Nightly observatory monitoring}
\label{results:monitoring}

Finally we demonstrate the score in the role we propose for it: a nightly image-quality
monitor. Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries} shows the visit-median score for all $92{,}460$
(visit, band) combinations over the full $246$-night baseline from 2025 April 24 to 2026 July
13, and Figure~\ref{fig:nightly} reduces the same data to one bar per night, the fraction of
raft-stacked PSFs in each quality tier.

The score varies strongly and coherently from night to night. Pooling all bands, the
fraction of PSFs in the HIGH or VERY HIGH tiers has a median over nights of $2.2\%$, but a
$10$th-to-$90$th percentile range of $0.0\%$ to $15.6\%$. The worst night, 2026 March 13,
has $28.9\%$ of its PSFs above the HIGH threshold, a factor of $13.1$ above the median night,
while several nights have none at all. This dynamic range is what makes the score
useful operationally: a bad night is unmistakable.

The structure is also coherent on longer timescales. Averaging the HIGH-plus-VERY-HIGH
fraction by month, we find $12.6\%$ in 2025 April, at the very start of LSSTCam
commissioning, falling to $0.5\%$ in 2025 October, rising again to $9.0$--$9.5\%$ through
2026 January to March, and falling to $3.3\%$ in 2026 April and to $1.2\%$, $0.9\%$ and
$0.5\%$ in May, June and July, the last weeks before the 2026 shutdown. We make no attempt
to attribute these episodes to specific engineering interventions, and have deliberately
not
cross-referenced the observatory's maintenance logs. What Figure~\ref{fig:nightly}
establishes is that the score responds on the timescales on which the optical system
actually changes, and with enough contrast to separate good periods from bad ones by eye. We
note that these figures pool all six bands, so part of the night-to-night variation reflects
which filters were used.

\subsection{Shapelet score versus moment score}
\label{results:score_comparison}

As set out in Section~\ref{method:moment_score}, the two scores share no machinery, so their
agreement is a genuine test. We match them per (visit, raft, band)
over the DP2 epoch, for which the per-raft moment catalog is available, taking the median
shapelet score of the PSFs in each raft. This yields $607{,}948$ matched measurements, shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison}.

We find that the two scores are strongly correlated, with a Spearman rank correlation of
$\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$ over the full matched sample, consistent across bands and ranging
only from $+0.908$ in $r$ to $+0.936$ in $u$ (Table~\ref{tab:rho}). The relation is close to
a power law over three decades, $\smom \propto \sshape^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 0.95$ and a
scatter of $0.24$ dex, that is, very nearly proportional, with
$\smom \approx 3.2\,\sshape$. As the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} shows, the
median relation is essentially identical in all six bands. This band-independence is a
useful practical property: a single threshold in either score means the same thing
regardless of filter.

A restricted score built only from the $n = 3$ modes, the closest shapelet analogue of the
third-order moment score, raises the agreement only slightly, to $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.933$;
the $n = 5$ modes therefore carry information largely redundant with the third-order moments
here. We retain them because they extend the dynamic range for the most strongly aberrated
PSFs, where third-order moments alone become a poor description of the profile.

The two scores are therefore close to interchangeable as rank-orderings of PSF asymmetry. In
practice the moment score is cheaper, since the pipeline already computes the moments for
every star, while the shapelet score is better behaved for strongly aberrated PSFs and
provides the full coefficient vector for diagnostics.

\section{CONCLUSION}
\label{conclusion:0}

We set out to build a PSF image-quality metric for the Rubin Observatory sensitive to the
asymmetric optical aberrations active-optics control exists to correct, and insensitive to
the atmosphere by construction rather than by calibration. We have presented two such
metrics, the shapelet score and the moment score, and measured them for $1{,}949{,}189$
raft-stacked PSFs from $94{,}422$ LSSTCam visits spanning 2025 April to 2026 July. Our main
findings are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Odd-$m$ shapelet modes are exactly blind to the atmosphere.} In a simulation
        of $1000$ point-symmetric atmospheric PSFs, every odd-$m$ coefficient sits at or
        below the double-precision floor, $\sigma \le 1.4 \times 10^{-16}$, while the
        even-$m$ coefficients vary by up to $7.1 \times 10^{-2}$. This follows from symmetry
        rather than the atmosphere model: any profile with
        $I(r,\theta) = I(r,\theta+\pi)$ has identically zero odd-$m$ power.
  \item \textbf{The score is close to independent of PSF size.} We find no appreciable
        correlation with the measured FWHM in any band or epoch. At fixed FWHM the score
        spans a $5$th-to-$95$th percentile range of $76$--$102$, while the median varies by
        only $1.9$--$2.7$ across the seeing range.
  \item \textbf{Two independent metrics agree.} The shapelet and third-order moment scores,
        which share no machinery, correlate at $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$ over $607{,}948$
        matched (visit, raft) measurements, with a near-proportional relation
        $\smom \approx 3.2\,\sshape$ that is the same in all six bands.
  \item \textbf{The score tracks real optical aberration.} Against the AOS corner wavefront
        sensors, an entirely independent instrument, the coma modes give the largest
        response to field spread, a factor $2.05$ and $2.35$ rise in median score between
        the lowest and highest quintile, against $1.10$ for the astigmatism mode $z_5$.
        Taken as magnitudes rather than field spreads, defocus is the strongest predictor
        of all, at $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.357$, and the score responds to coma only when the
        focus is poor.
  \item \textbf{The score is a practical nightly monitor.} The fraction of PSFs above the
        HIGH threshold has a median over nights of $2.2\%$ but ranges from $0\%$ to $28.9\%$,
        with coherent month-scale structure that separates good optical periods from bad
        ones by eye.
\end{enumerate}

Several caveats apply. Our PSFs are raft-level stacks drawn from a single detector per raft,
so the scores are insensitive to aberration varying within a raft and represent one field
position per raft rather than a raft average. The score is mildly conservative in poor
seeing (Section~\ref{results:fwhm}), and its negative correlation with the donut-blur FWHM
is not fully understood. Photon noise sets a
floor for shallow visits. The tier boundaries are round numbers justified only against
measured percentiles and visual inspection, with no input from any downstream science
requirement. Finally, the four-point spread we use for the corner-sensor Zernikes almost
certainly dilutes the correlations we report. We also caution that apparent correlations
between the score and slowly varying observing conditions are easily confounded with the
state of the optics, which itself varies on month timescales: an anticorrelation with the
wind speed that is clear when visits are pooled ($\rho_{\rm s} = -0.34$ in post-DP2)
vanishes within individual nights ($\rho_{\rm s} = -0.02$), because the most aberrated
months happened to be the calmest.

Several directions follow naturally. The most important is to quantify the impact of a
shapelet-score cut on weak-lensing systematics directly, through the $\rho$-statistics and
the two-point shear correlation function, which would turn the score from a monitoring
metric into a calibrated selection criterion; we defer this to a companion analysis. The
score can also be computed on the \textsc{Piff} PSF model, and the difference from the
star measurement would isolate how much of the asymmetry the model captures. The score is
already computed by the LSST Science Pipelines and published in the
\texttt{DetectorVisitProcessingSummary} table \cite{SDMSchemas}, in the two variants
described in Section~\ref{method:shapelet_score}. The \texttt{shapeletsOnlyIqScore} variant
is the closer analogue of the score defined here, although the mode sets still differ in
detail. Finally, applying the score per detector rather than per raft would map
the field dependence of the aberrations directly, at the cost of a higher noise floor.
