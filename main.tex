\begin{abstract}
Image quality at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory is usually summarised by the PSF full width
at half maximum and ellipticity, both second-moment quantities dominated by the atmosphere.
Neither is sensitive to the asymmetric optical aberrations that the active optics system
exists to correct, chiefly coma and trefoil, so a visit can be taken in excellent seeing and
still deliver a badly aberrated PSF. We present a metric sensitive to exactly those
aberrations
and blind to the atmosphere by construction. Decomposing each PSF into polar shapelets, we
show on simulated point-symmetric atmospheric PSFs that the coefficients with odd azimuthal
index $m$ vanish to double-precision round-off, while the even-$m$ coefficients absorb all
the atmospheric variation. We define the \emph{shapelet score} as the odd-$m$ fraction of
shapelet power, together with a companion \emph{moment score} built from third-order
adaptive moments, and measure both for
$1{,}468{,}222$ raft-stacked PSFs from $71{,}015$ LSSTCam visits taken between 2025 April
and 2026 May. The score is essentially independent of PSF size, spanning two orders of
magnitude at fixed seeing; it agrees with the moment score at a Spearman rank correlation of
$+0.923$; and it responds preferentially to the odd-$m$ Zernike aberrations measured by the
corner wavefront sensors, most strongly to coma. Used as a nightly monitor, the fraction of
PSFs it flags ranges from zero to $29\%$ between nights. We propose it as an image-quality
metric complementary to PSF size and to the existing AOS-derived metrics.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Rubin Observatory, LSST, point spread function, shapelets, image quality,
active optics, wavefront sensing}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{intro:0}
The NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory will carry out the Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(LSST), imaging the southern sky in six bands over ten years with the 3.2-gigapixel LSST
Camera (LSSTCam) on the 8.4\,m Simonyi Survey Telescope \cite{Ivezic2019}. Many of the
survey's headline science cases, and cosmic shear in particular, depend on how well we
understand the point spread function (PSF), which for a ground-based telescope is a
convolution of atmospheric turbulence, the optical system, and the detector. Errors in
modelling it propagate directly into galaxy shape measurements and hence into cosmological
parameter constraints \cite{HirataSeljak2003, Jarvis2021, Zhang2023}, so monitoring the
delivered PSF night by night and across the focal plane is both an operational requirement
and a prerequisite for the survey's precision-cosmology goals.

In practice, image quality is almost always summarised by second-moment quantities: the
full width at half maximum (FWHM) and the two components of the PSF ellipticity. These are
cheap to compute and capture the dominant behaviour of the PSF, but they are also dominated
by the atmosphere. Ground-layer seeing sets the FWHM and contributes most of the
ellipticity, so a visit taken through good seeing will report excellent second-moment image
quality even when the optical system is misaligned. The failure modes that the Rubin active
optics system (AOS) exists to correct are precisely those to which second moments are least
sensitive: coma, trefoil, and higher-order asymmetric aberrations from mirror figure error,
hexapod misalignment, or incomplete AOS convergence \cite{Xin2015}. A PSF can be compact,
nearly round, and still be badly aberrated. The observatory does track the wavefront
directly through Zernike coefficients estimated at the four corner wavefront sensors, but
those sample only the corners of the focal plane and come from a separate curvature-sensing
analysis external to the science images.

This motivates a metric built from the science images, sensitive to exactly the asymmetric
optical modes that second moments miss, and insensitive to the atmosphere by construction.
We decompose each PSF into a polar shapelet basis, the Gauss-Laguerre eigenfunctions of the
two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator
\cite{Refregier2003, MasseyRefregier2005, BernsteinJarvis2002}, and show, using a
simulated population of atmospheric PSFs, that the coefficients split cleanly into those
that respond strongly to atmospheric size and ellipticity variation and those that do not
respond at all. The second group is exactly the set of modes with odd azimuthal index $m$,
which vanish identically for any point-symmetric profile. We define the \emph{shapelet
score} as the fraction of total shapelet power carried by those modes, and a companion
\emph{moment score} built from the third-order adaptive moments of the same stars. Both are
dimensionless, computed per PSF, and require no external calibration.

We measure these scores for $1{,}468{,}222$ raft-stacked PSFs drawn from $71{,}015$ LSSTCam
visits spanning 2025 April to 2026 May, covering both the Data Preview 2 (DP2) processing
\cite{DP2} and subsequent nightly validation. We propose the shapelet score as a complement
to the PSF size and AOS-derived metrics already in use, rather than a replacement for them.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{data:0} describes the LSSTCam data and the
auxiliary catalogs. Section~\ref{method:0} sets out the shapelet formalism, presents the
atmospheric simulation that motivates the mode selection, and defines both scores.
Section~\ref{results:0} presents the measured distributions, validates the scores visually
and against the higher-order moments, and demonstrates their behaviour as a function of
observing conditions and of time. We conclude in Section~\ref{conclusion:0}.

\subsection{PSF star stamps}
\label{data:stamps}

We build our PSF sample from cutouts of the stars the pipeline used to constrain the PSF
model: the \texttt{refit\_psf\_star} catalog for DP2, and the \texttt{single\_visit\_star}
catalog restricted to \texttt{calib\_psf\_used = True} for post-DP2. Stamps are cut directly
from the \texttt{preliminary\_visit\_image} pixels at the catalog centroids. We restrict the
analysis to the 21 science rafts, excluding the four corner rafts (\texttt{R00},
\texttt{R04}, \texttt{R40}, \texttt{R44}) which carry the guide and wavefront sensors.

For each visit and each science raft we select one detector at random, draw up to 100 stars
from it, cut a $51 \times 51$ pixel ($10.2\arcsec$ on a side at the $0.2\arcsec$ pixel
scale) stamp around each, normalise each to unit flux, and average them, rejecting stars
whose stamps would cross a detector edge. This yields one stacked PSF image per raft per
visit, up to 21 per visit, with a median of $60$ contributing stars.

We stack for two reasons. First, averaging $\sim\!10^2$ flux-normalised stars suppresses
photon noise by an order of magnitude, so the high-order coefficients are measured against a
much reduced noise floor. Second, a raft is the natural unit for the aberrations we are
trying to detect: coma and trefoil vary smoothly across the focal plane on scales much
larger than a single detector, whereas the atmospheric contribution is common to the whole
focal plane within a visit. Two consequences follow: stacking suppresses aberration varying
\emph{within} a raft, so our scores are a lower bound on the true per-star asymmetry, and
drawing from one detector means a stamp samples one quadrant of the raft rather than its
mean field position.

\subsection{Higher-order moment catalog}
\label{data:moments}

For the moment score of Section~\ref{method:moment_score} we use the adaptive-moment
measurements produced by the LSST Science Pipelines for the same PSF stars. Third-order
moments come from the \texttt{ext\_shapeHSM\_HigherOrderMomentsSource} plugin, which
measures the normalised moments $M_{pq}$ in the coordinate frame whitened by the HSM
adaptive second moments \cite{HirataSeljak2003, BernsteinJarvis2002}, making them
dimensionless \cite{Zhang2023}. From the per-star measurements we form, for each visit, the
median of every moment over the stars in each science raft, giving a per-(visit, raft) table
matched to the stamp sample described above.

\subsection{Wavefront and observing-condition metadata}
\label{data:zernike}

We take three classes of auxiliary metadata from the Consolidated Database (consDB), matched
to our visits by \texttt{visit\_id}.

\textbf{Wavefront Zernikes.} The Rubin AOS estimates the wavefront by curvature sensing on
the four corner wavefront sensors \cite{Xin2015}. From the \texttt{ccdvisit1\_quicklook}
table we retrieve the annular Zernike coefficients $z_4$ through $z_{11}$ at each corner in
the Noll ordering \cite{Noll1976}, covering defocus, astigmatism, coma, trefoil and
spherical, and form the peak-to-peak spread $\max_j z_i^{(j)} - \min_j z_i^{(j)}$ over the
four corner positions $j$ as a proxy for the aberration gradient across the field.

\textbf{Delivered image quality.} We use \texttt{aos\_fwhm}, the AOS estimate of the
delivered FWHM, and \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}, the FWHM contribution inferred from the
donut images on the wavefront sensors. Both are independent of our shapelet measurement and
provide the comparison baseline in Section~\ref{results:conditions}.

\textbf{Sky background.} We use \texttt{sky\_bg}, the median across detectors within a
visit, as a proxy for the photon noise level in the stamps.

\section{PSF QUALITY SCORES}
\label{method:0}

In this section we define the two scores. Section~\ref{method:shapelet} sets out the
shapelet formalism; Section~\ref{method:atm} uses a simulated atmospheric PSF population to
determine which modes respond to the atmosphere, the measurement that motivates the
definition; Sections~\ref{method:shapelet_score} and \ref{method:moment_score} define the
two scores; and Section~\ref{method:tiers} gives the quality tiers.

\subsection{Shapelet decomposition of the PSF}
\label{method:shapelet}

Shapelets are the eigenfunctions of the two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator, a
complete orthonormal basis in which a localised image can be expanded to a chosen maximum
order \cite{Refregier2003, BernsteinJarvis2002}. We use the polar (Gauss--Laguerre) form
\cite{MasseyRefregier2005}, whose basis functions carry a definite azimuthal dependence
$e^{im\theta}$ and therefore separate the image into components of explicit rotational
symmetry. This is the property we exploit throughout.

We write the surface brightness of a PSF as

\begin{equation}
I(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{pq} b_{pq}\,
               \psi_{pq}\!\left(\frac{r}{\sigma}, \theta\right),
\label{eq:shapelet}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is an overall scale length, $b_{pq}$ are the shapelet coefficients, and the
basis functions are

\begin{equation}
\psi_{pq}(r, \theta) = \frac{(-1)^q}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sqrt{\frac{q!}{p!}}\;
                       r^{m} e^{im\theta} e^{-r^2/2} L_q^{(m)}(r^2),
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}

with $L_q^{(m)}$ the generalised Laguerre polynomials. The indices $p$ and $q$ count quanta
of positive and negative angular momentum; we label modes instead by the order $n = p + q$
and the azimuthal index $m = p - q$, so that $n$ and $m$ always have the same parity.
Requiring $I(r,\theta)$ to be real imposes $b_{pq} = b_{qp}^{*}$, so each $m > 0$ mode
contributes one real and one imaginary coefficient and each $m = 0$ mode a single real one.

We truncate at $n \le 6$, which gives the $28$ coefficients shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:basis}. We fit each stacked PSF stamp using \textsc{GalSim}
\cite{Rowe2015}: we measure the adaptive moments with \texttt{FindAdaptiveMom}, which
implements the HSM algorithm \cite{HirataSeljak2003}, adopt the resulting
$\sigma_{\rm HSM}$ (in arcseconds) as the shapelet scale $\sigma$, and solve the linear
least-squares problem for $b_{pq}$ with \texttt{Shapelet.fit} in surface-brightness
normalisation. Setting the scale from the adaptive moments is what makes the decomposition
approximately scale-free, so the coefficients measure PSF \emph{shape} rather than PSF size.
Throughout, we quote FWHM as $2.3548\,\sigma_{\rm HSM}$.

\subsection{Which modes respond to the atmosphere?}
\label{method:atm}

Which of the 28 coefficients carry information about the optics, and which merely track the
atmosphere? We answer this with a simulation.

We generate $1000$ realisations of an atmospheric PSF, each the sum of two Moffat profiles
with independently drawn parameters: FWHM uniform on $[0.5, 1.2]\arcsec$, Moffat $\beta$
uniform on $[2.5, 4.5]$, and shear components $g_1, g_2$ drawn from a normal distribution of
width $0.03$ and clipped at $|g| < 0.3$. The two-component sum gives more freedom than a
single Moffat, so the population spans a realistic range of size, ellipticity, and radial
profile shape. Crucially, each component is centred at the origin, so every realisation is
point-symmetric: the population contains atmospheric variation and, by construction, no
optical asymmetry whatsoever. We fit each with the same procedure used for the data,
recovering all $1000$ fits with measured FWHM spanning $0.56$--$1.32\arcsec$.

Figure~\ref{fig:atm} shows the result, and it is unambiguous. The upper panel gives the
standard deviation of each coefficient across realisations. The even-$m$ coefficients vary
substantially, the largest being $(0,0)$ at $\sigma = 7.1 \times 10^{-2}$, and together
carry $99.96\%$ of the median total power. Every odd-$m$ coefficient has
$\sigma \le 1.4 \times 10^{-16}$: zero at the level of double-precision round-off, a ratio
of $5 \times 10^{14}$ between the largest even-$m$ and odd-$m$ variation.

This follows from symmetry alone, and therefore holds for any atmosphere model. A profile
that satisfies $I(r, \theta) = I(r, \theta + \pi)$ has, by the orthogonality of
$e^{im\theta}$, identically zero projection onto every basis function with odd $m$. Any
point-symmetric PSF therefore has no odd-$m$ power at all, whether a Gaussian, a Moffat, a
Kolmogorov profile, an elliptical version of any of these, or the PSF of a perfectly aligned
telescope. Odd-$m$ power can only be produced by a genuine asymmetry.

The lower panel makes the same point in terms of correlations with the input parameters: the
even-$m$ coefficients correlate with the atmospheric FWHM at up to $|r| = 0.45$, whereas the
odd-$m$ coefficients show no appreciable correlation with either input. Their residual
values are set by floating-point arithmetic in the fit.

\subsection{The shapelet score}
\label{method:shapelet_score}

Motivated by Section~\ref{method:atm}, we define the shapelet score as the fraction of the
total shapelet power carried by the odd-$m$ modes:

\begin{equation}
\sshape = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n \geq 3,\; m\ \mathrm{odd}} |b_{nm}|^2}
               {\displaystyle\sum_{nm} |b_{nm}|^2} .
\label{eq:shapelet_score}
\end{equation}

At $n \le 6$ the numerator runs over five modes, $b_{33}$, $b_{31}$, $b_{55}$, $b_{53}$ and
$b_{51}$, or ten of the 28 real coefficients once real and imaginary parts are counted
separately. The score is dimensionless, bounded in $[0, 1]$, and rotationally invariant,
since rotating by $\phi$ multiplies $b_{nm}$ by $e^{im\phi}$ and leaves $|b_{nm}|^2$
unchanged. Normalising by the total power makes it independent of the stamp flux
normalisation, and, because $\sigma$ is set by the adaptive moments, largely independent of
PSF size. We test this last claim directly in Section~\ref{results:fwhm}.

Two exclusions deserve comment. First, we exclude the $n = 1$ modes, which are odd in $m$
but describe a displacement of the profile rather than a distortion of it: a stamp whose
centroid is offset from the fitted centre acquires $n = 1$ power without being asymmetric,
so including them would make the score sensitive to stamp registration. This matters in
practice, since in the $i$-band data the $n = 1$ modes carry a median fractional power of
$1.8 \times 10^{-4}$, roughly a quarter of the typical score. Second, we truncate at
$n \le 6$; higher orders would add further odd-$m$ modes, but at fixed stamp size and noise
level they are increasingly poorly constrained, and we find $n \le 6$ already captures the
asymmetries visible by eye (Section~\ref{results:gallery}).

On the simulated atmospheric population of Section~\ref{method:atm} the score has a median
of $1.3 \times 10^{-32}$, some $29$ orders of magnitude below the smallest threshold we will
use on real data. Any measured score is therefore attributable to the optics, the detector,
or noise. Seeing alone cannot produce one.

\subsection{The moment score}
\label{method:moment_score}

We construct a second, independent metric from the third-order adaptive moments of
Section~\ref{data:moments}, both as a cross-check and because moments are already computed
by the pipeline for every star. Following Ref.~\citenum{Zhang2023}, we form the spin-1 and
spin-3 combinations of the normalised third moments $M_{pq}$,

\begin{align}
c_{11} &= M_{30} + M_{12},  &  c_{12} &= M_{21} + M_{03}, \label{eq:coma}\\
c_{31} &= M_{30} - 3 M_{12}, &  c_{32} &= 3 M_{21} - M_{03}, \label{eq:trefoil}
\end{align}

where $(c_{11}, c_{12})$ transform as spin-1 under rotation and describe the coma-like
asymmetry of the PSF, and $(c_{31}, c_{32})$ transform as spin-3 and describe the
trefoil-like asymmetry. The moment score is the weighted sum of their squared moduli,

\begin{equation}
\smom = 3\left(c_{11}^2 + c_{12}^2\right) + \left(c_{31}^2 + c_{32}^2\right),
\label{eq:moment_score}
\end{equation}

which, like the shapelet score, is dimensionless and rotationally invariant. The factor of
three up-weights the coma term; we adopt it for consistency with the metric already in use
for observatory monitoring, and note that the choice affects the normalisation of $\smom$
but not strongly its rank ordering, since coma and trefoil are themselves correlated.

The two scores capture the same physical information, the asymmetric odd-$m$ content of the
PSF, but reach it by completely different routes: a least-squares basis expansion of the
image, versus weighted pixel moments. They share no machinery. We quantify their agreement
in Section~\ref{results:score_comparison}.

\subsection{Score tiers}
\label{method:tiers}

For operational use it is convenient to reduce the score to a small number of quality
classes. We adopt four tiers: \textbf{LOW} ($\sshape < 0.002$), clean, symmetric PSFs;
\textbf{MEDIUM} ($0.002 \le \sshape < 0.005$), mild asymmetry; \textbf{HIGH}
($0.005 \le \sshape < 0.02$), clearly visible coma or trefoil; and \textbf{VERY HIGH}
($\sshape \ge 0.02$), strongly aberrated PSFs. These are round numbers chosen to sit near
the $80$th, $95$th and $99$th percentiles of the global distribution, justified against the
measured percentiles in Section~\ref{results:distributions} and against visual inspection in
Section~\ref{results:gallery}.

\section{RESULTS}
\label{results:0}

In this section we present the measured scores. Section~\ref{results:distributions} gives
the score distributions and uses them to justify the tier boundaries,
Section~\ref{results:fwhm} tests the claim that the score is independent of PSF size, and
Section~\ref{results:gallery} validates the tiers visually.
Section~\ref{results:score_comparison} compares the two scores.
Sections~\ref{results:conditions} and \ref{results:optics} examine the dependence on
observing conditions and on the AOS wavefront estimates, and
Section~\ref{results:monitoring} demonstrates the score as a nightly monitoring tool.

Throughout we compare quantities measured over samples of $10^4$ to $10^5$ visits. At these
sample sizes a correlation coefficient of even a few hundredths is formally distinguishable
from zero while carrying almost no practical information about an individual visit. We
therefore quote correlation coefficients only where they reach $|\rho_{\rm s}| \gtrsim 0.3$,
and describe weaker relationships qualitatively; where we report no appreciable correlation,
the measured coefficient is below that threshold.

\subsection{Score distributions}
\label{results:distributions}

Figure~\ref{fig:dist} shows the distribution of the shapelet score in each band, for both
epochs, and Table~\ref{tab:percentiles} lists the corresponding percentiles. The
distribution is broad and strongly right-skewed: it peaks near $S_{\rm shapelet} \approx
10^{-4}$ and extends over more than two decades, with a long tail above $10^{-2}$. This
shape is what makes the score useful: most PSFs are clean, and the metric spends its
dynamic range on the minority that are not.

The distributions are similar in all six bands but shift systematically to higher scores
with wavelength: in DP2 the median rises from $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$ in $u$ to $4.9 \times
10^{-4}$ in $y$. Two plausible contributions, which we do not attempt to separate here, are
that the redder bands see better seeing, so a fixed optical aberration represents a larger
fraction of the total PSF power, and that the $u$ band has the fewest and faintest stars per
stamp, so its scores are the most affected by noise. Comparing epochs, post-DP2 is shifted
to modestly higher scores in $i$, $z$ and $y$, the $i$-band median rising from
$4.0 \times 10^{-4}$ to $7.6 \times 10^{-4}$, while $u$ improves. We caution against
reading this as a simple degradation, since post-DP2 covers a period of active work on the
telescope optics, and Section~\ref{results:monitoring} shows that most of it is driven by a
limited number of nights rather than a uniform shift.

The measured distributions bear out the tier boundaries of Section~\ref{method:tiers}.
Pooling all bands, $S_{\rm shapelet} = 0.002$, $0.005$ and $0.02$ fall at the $87.1$st,
$95.5$th and $99.5$th percentiles in DP2 and the $79.6$th, $93.5$th and $99.4$th in
post-DP2, giving tier occupancies of $87.1\%$ LOW, $8.4\%$ MEDIUM, $4.0\%$ HIGH and $0.5\%$
VERY HIGH for DP2, and $79.6\%$, $13.9\%$, $5.9\%$ and $0.6\%$ for post-DP2. We note that
fixing the boundaries at absolute values rather than percentiles is deliberate: a
percentile-based threshold would by construction flag the same fraction of visits every
night and could not register a genuine change in image quality.

\subsection{Independence from PSF size}
\label{results:fwhm}

A metric intended to complement the FWHM is only useful if it carries information the FWHM
does not. Figure~\ref{fig:fwhm} tests this directly, showing the $i$-band shapelet score
against the measured PSF FWHM for both epochs.

We find no appreciable correlation between the two, in any band or either epoch. The running
median, the orange line, is close to flat: in DP2 it varies from
$5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ at $0.6$--$0.8\arcsec$ to $3.8 \times 10^{-4}$ at $1.2$--$1.5\arcsec$,
a change of only $30\%$ across the full useful seeing range.

The more important point is the vertical extent of the distribution. Averaged over bins
between $0.6\arcsec$ and $1.5\arcsec$, the ratio of the $95$th to the $5$th percentile at
fixed FWHM is $84$ in DP2 and $124$ in post-DP2, whereas the median varies by only a factor
of $1.3$ and $3.1$ over the same range. The scatter at fixed seeing is therefore one to two
orders of magnitude larger than any trend with seeing: knowing the FWHM of a visit tells one
almost nothing about whether its PSF is aberrated, and PSFs in the HIGH and VERY HIGH tiers
are found at all seeing values, including the best.

We do note a real, if weak, effect in the post-DP2 panel, where the median score falls by a
factor of $\sim\!3$ between $0.8\arcsec$ and $1.5\arcsec$. We interpret this as dilution
rather than a size dependence of the aberration: because the score is a \emph{fraction} of
total power, a larger atmospheric contribution increases the denominator and suppresses the
score at fixed aberration. This makes the score mildly conservative in poor seeing, in the
opposite direction to any trend that would mimic aberration.

\subsection{Visual validation by tier}
\label{results:gallery}

Any quality metric should be checked against what the images actually look like.
Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} shows three $i$-band raft-stacked PSFs from each tier, with the
residual after subtracting the fitted shapelet model. For LOW and VERY HIGH we show the
extremes of the distribution, and for the two intermediate tiers PSFs near the geometric
midpoint, so that the figure spans the full visual range of the metric.

The progression is clear by eye. LOW-tier stamps are round and compact, with residuals
consistent with noise; MEDIUM-tier stamps show mild elongation and faint residual structure;
HIGH-tier stamps display an unmistakable comatic profile, a displaced, asymmetric core with
a one-sided flare, together with coherent, large-scale residual structure;
and VERY HIGH-tier stamps are strongly aberrated, one showing the double-lobed morphology
characteristic of a large misalignment term.

The annotated moment scores track the tiers independently, with ranges disjoint across all
four: $\smom$ spans $2.7 \times 10^{-6}$ to $3.8 \times 10^{-5}$ for the LOW examples,
$0.0076$--$0.0154$ for MEDIUM, $0.0376$--$0.0525$ for HIGH and $0.095$--$0.236$ for VERY
HIGH. Since the moment score shares no machinery with the shapelet fit, this ordering is a
non-trivial consistency check. The annotated FWHM values, by contrast, are interleaved
across tiers. The LOW examples have the \emph{largest} FWHM of any row, at
$1.17$--$1.84\arcsec$, against $0.92$--$1.36\arcsec$ for HIGH, reinforcing the conclusion
of Section~\ref{results:fwhm} that PSF size and PSF asymmetry are close to independent.

\subsection{Shapelet score versus moment score}
\label{results:score_comparison}

As set out in Section~\ref{method:moment_score}, the two scores share no machinery, so their
agreement is a genuine test. We match them per (visit, raft, band)
over the DP2 epoch, for which the per-raft moment catalog is available, taking the median
shapelet score of the PSFs in each raft. This yields $607{,}948$ matched measurements, shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison}.

We find that the two scores are strongly correlated, with a Spearman rank correlation of
$\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$ over the full matched sample, consistent across bands and ranging
only from $+0.908$ in $r$ to $+0.936$ in $u$ (Table~\ref{tab:rho}). The relation is close to
a power law over three decades, $\smom \propto \sshape^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 0.95$ and a
scatter of $0.24$ dex, that is, very nearly proportional, with
$\smom \approx 3.2\,\sshape$. As the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} shows, the
median relation is essentially identical in all six bands. This band-independence is a
useful practical property: a single threshold in either score means the same thing
regardless of filter.

A restricted score built only from the $n = 3$ modes, the closest shapelet analogue of the
third-order moment score, raises the agreement only slightly, to $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.933$;
the $n = 5$ modes therefore carry information largely redundant with the third-order moments
here. We retain them because they extend the dynamic range for the most strongly aberrated
PSFs, where third-order moments alone become a poor description of the profile.

The two scores are therefore close to interchangeable as rank-orderings of PSF asymmetry. In
practice the moment score is cheaper, since the pipeline already computes the moments for
every star, while the shapelet score is better behaved for strongly aberrated PSFs and
provides the full coefficient vector for diagnostics.

\subsection{Dependence on observing conditions}
\label{results:conditions}

We next ask whether the score is driven by anything other than the optics. In
Figure~\ref{fig:conditions} we plot the visit-median score against three quantities
available from consDB for every visit: the sky background, the AOS estimate of the delivered
FWHM (\texttt{aos\_fwhm}), and the donut-blur FWHM (\texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}).

We find no appreciable correlation with the sky background in either epoch. A weak positive
trend is expected, since a brighter sky means noisier stamps and photon noise projects onto
the odd-$m$ modes as well as the rest; this sets a noise floor for the shallowest visits,
and is one reason we stack up to $100$ stars per stamp.

The two AOS quantities behave very differently, and understanding why is instructive. The
score correlates \emph{positively} with \texttt{aos\_fwhm} ($\rho_{\rm s} = +0.45$ in DP2,
and more weakly in post-DP2) but \emph{negatively} with \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}
($\rho_{\rm s} = -0.33$ and $-0.57$). At first sight the negative sign is puzzling, since a
larger donut blur should indicate a larger wavefront error and therefore a more aberrated
PSF. The explanation is the dilution effect identified in Section~\ref{results:fwhm}:
\texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} is very nearly a proxy for the delivered image size, correlating
with the measured PSF FWHM at $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.81$ in DP2 and $+0.73$ in post-DP2, whereas
\texttt{aos\_fwhm} tracks it much more weakly ($+0.37$). Because the score is a fraction of
total power, anything that raises the atmospheric contribution lowers it at fixed
aberration. The negative correlation therefore reflects seeing dilution viewed through a
seeing proxy, and does not imply that larger wavefront errors produce rounder PSFs. The
positive correlation with \texttt{aos\_fwhm}, much less contaminated by seeing, is in the
expected direction. We caution that we have taken both consDB quantities at face value without
tracing their definitions through the AOS pipeline, so this interpretation rests on the
measured correlations.

\subsection{Link to optical aberration}
\label{results:optics}

The strongest test of whether the shapelet score measures optical aberration is to compare
it against an independent optical measurement. The four corner wavefront sensors provide
exactly that, estimating $z_4$--$z_{11}$ from curvature sensing and using neither the
science pixels nor any part of our analysis.

Figure~\ref{fig:zernike} shows how the visit-median score responds to each of these eight
field spreads, and the ordering follows the prediction of the mode selection remarkably
closely. Between the lowest and the highest quintile of field spread, the median shapelet
score rises by a factor of $2.35$ for $z_8$ and $2.05$ for $z_7$, by $1.53$ and $1.40$ for
$z_{10}$ and $z_9$, and by only $1.10$ for $z_5$. The same ordering appears in the rank
correlations in the left panel, where the two coma modes are the only ones to reach
$\rho_{\rm s} \simeq +0.17$ and $+0.20$.

This ordering is the point. Coma and trefoil are the odd-$m$ Zernike aberrations, with
$m = 1$ and $m = 3$ respectively, and are precisely those capable of producing odd-$m$
shapelet power, whereas defocus, astigmatism and spherical aberration have even $m$ and
produce distortions the score is constructed to ignore. Averaged over modes, the odd-$m$ Zernikes
give a mean rank correlation about twice that of the even-$m$ ones. The absolute
correlations are small, for reasons we return to below; it is their relative ordering, which
matches the symmetry argument of Section~\ref{method:atm} with no free parameters, that we
regard as meaningful.

We note two limitations. First, the odd-$m$/even-$m$ separation is not perfectly clean: the
astigmatism mode $z_6$ gives a ratio of $1.42$, comparable to the trefoil modes, and we have
no explanation for the asymmetry between $z_5$ and $z_6$. Second, and more fundamentally,
the corner sensors sample the wavefront at only four field positions, all outside the
science rafts on which the score is measured, so the four-point spread is a crude proxy for
the true field dependence and we expect it to dilute the correlations substantially. That a
clear, correctly-ordered signal survives this mismatch is the significant result.

\subsection{Nightly observatory monitoring}
\label{results:monitoring}

Finally we demonstrate the score in the role we propose for it: a nightly image-quality
monitor. Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries} shows the visit-median score for all $69{,}053$
(visit, band) combinations over the full $209$-night baseline from 2025 April 24 to 2026 May
13, and Figure~\ref{fig:nightly} reduces the same data to one bar per night, the fraction of
raft-stacked PSFs in each quality tier.

The score varies strongly and coherently from night to night. Pooling all bands, the
fraction of PSFs in the HIGH or VERY HIGH tiers has a median over nights of $3.2\%$, but a
$10$th-to-$90$th percentile range of $0.1\%$ to $16.4\%$. The worst night, 2026 March 13,
has $28.9\%$ of its PSFs above the HIGH threshold, a factor of $9.2$ above the median night,
while several nights have none at all. This dynamic range is what makes the score
useful operationally: a bad night is unmistakable.

The structure is also coherent on longer timescales. Averaging the HIGH-plus-VERY-HIGH
fraction by month, we find $12.6\%$ in 2025 April, at the very start of LSSTCam
commissioning, falling to $0.5\%$ in 2025 October, rising again to $9.0$--$9.5\%$ through
2026 January to March, and falling to $2.4\%$ by 2026 May. We make no attempt to attribute
these episodes to specific engineering interventions, and have deliberately not
cross-referenced the observatory's maintenance logs. What Figure~\ref{fig:nightly}
establishes is that the score responds on the timescales on which the optical system
actually changes, and with enough contrast to separate good periods from bad ones by eye. We
note that these figures pool all six bands, so part of the night-to-night variation reflects
which filters were used.

\section{CONCLUSION}
\label{conclusion:0}

We set out to build a PSF image-quality metric for the Rubin Observatory sensitive to the
asymmetric optical aberrations active-optics control exists to correct, and insensitive to
the atmosphere by construction rather than by calibration. We have presented two such
metrics, the shapelet score and the moment score, and measured them for $1{,}468{,}222$
raft-stacked PSFs from $71{,}015$ LSSTCam visits spanning 2025 April to 2026 May. Our main
findings are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Odd-$m$ shapelet modes are exactly blind to the atmosphere.} In a simulation
        of $1000$ point-symmetric atmospheric PSFs, every odd-$m$ coefficient sits at or
        below the double-precision floor, $\sigma \le 1.4 \times 10^{-16}$, while the
        even-$m$ coefficients vary by up to $7.1 \times 10^{-2}$. This follows from symmetry
        rather than the atmosphere model: any profile with
        $I(r,\theta) = I(r,\theta+\pi)$ has identically zero odd-$m$ power.
  \item \textbf{The score is close to independent of PSF size.} We find no appreciable
        correlation with the measured FWHM in any band or epoch. At fixed FWHM the score
        spans a $5$th-to-$95$th percentile range of $84$--$124$, while the median varies by
        only $1.3$--$3.1$ across the seeing range.
  \item \textbf{Two independent metrics agree.} The shapelet and third-order moment scores,
        which share no machinery, correlate at $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$ over $607{,}948$
        matched (visit, raft) measurements, with a near-proportional relation
        $\smom \approx 3.2\,\sshape$ that is the same in all six bands.
  \item \textbf{The score tracks real optical aberration.} Against the AOS corner wavefront
        sensors, an entirely independent instrument, the score responds most strongly
        to the odd-$m$ Zernikes: $z_7$ and $z_8$ give a factor $2.05$ and $2.35$ rise in
        median score between the lowest and highest quintile of field spread, against
        $1.10$ for the astigmatism mode $z_5$.
  \item \textbf{The score is a practical nightly monitor.} The fraction of PSFs above the
        HIGH threshold has a median over nights of $3.2\%$ but ranges from $0\%$ to $28.9\%$,
        with coherent month-scale structure that separates good optical periods from bad
        ones by eye.
\end{enumerate}

Several caveats apply. Our PSFs are raft-level stacks drawn from a single detector per raft,
so the scores are insensitive to aberration varying within a raft and represent one field
position per raft rather than a raft average. The score is a \emph{fraction} of total power,
which makes it mildly conservative in poor seeing (Section~\ref{results:fwhm}); a variant
normalised by an external size estimate would remove this dilution. Photon noise sets a
floor for shallow visits. The tier boundaries are round numbers justified only against
measured percentiles and visual inspection, with no input from any downstream science
requirement. Finally, the four-point spread we use for the corner-sensor Zernikes almost
certainly dilutes the correlations we report.

Several directions follow naturally. The most important is to quantify the impact of a
shapelet-score cut on weak-lensing systematics directly, through the $\rho$-statistics and
the two-point shear correlation function, which would turn the score from a monitoring
metric into a calibrated selection criterion; we defer this to a companion analysis. The
score can also be computed on the \textsc{Piff} PSF model, and the difference from the star
measurement would isolate how much of the asymmetry the model captures. A variant has
already been adopted into the LSST Science Pipelines as \texttt{shapeletsIqScore}, with a
mode set differing slightly from the definition used here. Finally, applying the score per
detector rather than per raft would map the field dependence of the aberrations directly, at
the cost of a higher noise floor.
