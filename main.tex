\begin{abstract}
Image quality at the Vera C. Rubin Observatory is usually summarised by the PSF full width
at half maximum and ellipticity. Both are second-moment quantities, and both are dominated
by the atmosphere, so neither is sensitive to the asymmetric optical aberrations --- coma
and trefoil --- that the active optics system exists to correct. A visit can be taken in
excellent seeing and still deliver a badly aberrated PSF. We present an image-quality metric
that is sensitive to exactly those aberrations and blind to the atmosphere by construction.
Decomposing each PSF into polar shapelets, we show using a simulated population of
point-symmetric atmospheric PSFs that the coefficients with odd azimuthal index $m$ vanish
identically, at the level of double-precision round-off, while the even-$m$ coefficients
absorb all of the atmospheric variation. We define the \emph{shapelet score} as the fraction
of shapelet power carried by the odd-$m$ modes, together with a companion \emph{moment
score} built from third-order adaptive moments. We measure both for $1{,}468{,}222$
raft-stacked PSFs from $71{,}015$ LSSTCam visits taken between 2025 April and 2026 May,
covering the Data Preview 2 processing and the subsequent nightly validation. The shapelet
score is essentially independent of PSF size, correlating with FWHM at $|r| < 0.20$ while
spanning two orders of magnitude at fixed seeing; it agrees with the independently
constructed moment score at a Spearman rank correlation of $+0.923$; and it responds
preferentially to the odd-$m$ Zernike aberrations measured by the corner wavefront sensors,
most strongly to coma. Used as a nightly monitor, the fraction of PSFs it flags varies from
zero to $29\%$ between nights, with coherent month-scale structure. We propose the shapelet
score as an image-quality metric complementary to PSF size and to the existing AOS-derived
metrics.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Rubin Observatory, LSST, point spread function, shapelets, image quality,
active optics, wavefront sensing}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{intro:0}
The NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory will carry out the Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(LSST), imaging the southern sky in six bands over ten years with the 3.2-gigapixel LSST
Camera (LSSTCam) on the 8.4\,m Simonyi Survey Telescope \cite{Ivezic2019}. Many of the
survey's headline science cases --- and cosmic shear in particular --- depend on how well
we understand the point spread function (PSF). The PSF delivered by a ground-based
telescope is a convolution of atmospheric turbulence, the optical system, and the detector,
and errors in modelling it propagate directly into galaxy shape measurements and hence into
cosmological parameter constraints \cite{HirataSeljak2003, Jarvis2021, Zhang2023}.
Monitoring the quality of the delivered PSF, night by night and across the focal plane, is
therefore both an operational requirement for the observatory and a prerequisite for the
survey's precision-cosmology goals.

In practice, image quality is almost always summarised by second-moment quantities: the
full width at half maximum (FWHM) and the two components of the PSF ellipticity. These are
natural choices, they are cheap to compute, and they capture the dominant behaviour of the
PSF. They are also, however, dominated by the atmosphere. Ground-layer seeing sets the FWHM
and contributes most of the ellipticity, so a visit taken through good seeing will report
excellent second-moment image quality even when the optical system is misaligned. The
failure modes that the Rubin active optics system (AOS) exists to correct --- coma,
trefoil, and higher-order asymmetric aberrations arising from mirror figure error, hexapod
misalignment, or incomplete AOS convergence \cite{Xin2015} --- are precisely the modes to
which second moments are least sensitive. A PSF can be compact, nearly round, and still be
badly aberrated. We note that the observatory does track wavefront information directly,
through the Zernike coefficients estimated at the four corner wavefront sensors, but those
estimates sample only the corners of the focal plane and are derived from a separate
curvature-sensing analysis rather than from the science images themselves.

This motivates a metric that is built from the science images, is sensitive to exactly the
asymmetric optical modes that second moments miss, and is insensitive to the atmosphere by
construction. In this work we present such a metric. We decompose each PSF into a polar
shapelet basis \cite{Refregier2003, MasseyRefregier2005, BernsteinJarvis2002} --- the
Gauss-Laguerre eigenfunctions of the two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator --- and
show, using a simulated population of atmospheric PSFs, that the shapelet coefficients
split cleanly into two groups: those that respond strongly to atmospheric size and
ellipticity variation, and those that do not respond at all. The second group is exactly
the set of modes with odd azimuthal index $m$, which vanish identically for any
point-symmetric profile. We define the \emph{shapelet score} as the fraction of total
shapelet power carried by those odd-$m$ modes, and a companion \emph{moment score} built
from the third-order adaptive moments of the same stars. Both are dimensionless, both are
computed per PSF, and neither requires any external calibration.

We measure these scores for $1{,}468{,}222$ raft-stacked PSFs drawn from $71{,}015$ LSSTCam
visits spanning 2025 April to 2026 May, covering both the Data Preview 2 (DP2) processing
\cite{DP2} and the subsequent nightly-validation processing. We show that the shapelet
score is essentially independent of the PSF size, that it correlates strongly with the
independently measured moment score, that it responds to AOS wavefront Zernike gradients
across the focal plane, and that it isolates nights and visits with visibly aberrated PSFs
that standard FWHM-based metrics do not flag. We therefore propose the shapelet score as an
image-quality metric that is complementary to --- rather than a replacement for --- PSF
size and the AOS-derived metrics already in use at the observatory.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section~\ref{data:0} we describe the LSSTCam data,
the PSF stamp extraction, and the auxiliary moment and wavefront catalogs. In
Section~\ref{method:0} we set out the shapelet formalism, present the atmospheric
simulation that motivates the mode selection, and define both scores. In
Section~\ref{results:0} we present the measured score distributions, validate the scores
visually and against the higher-order moments, and demonstrate their behaviour as a
function of observing conditions and of time. We conclude in Section~\ref{conclusion:0}.

\subsection{PSF star stamps}
\label{data:stamps}

We build our PSF sample from image cutouts of stars that the pipeline used to constrain the
PSF model. For DP2 we select stars from the \texttt{refit\_psf\_star} catalog; for post-DP2
we use the \texttt{single\_visit\_star} catalog restricted to
\texttt{calib\_psf\_used = True}. In both cases we take the star centroids from
\texttt{slot\_Centroid\_x} and \texttt{slot\_Centroid\_y}, and cut stamps directly from the
\texttt{preliminary\_visit\_image} pixels.

We restrict the analysis to the 21 science rafts, excluding the four corner rafts
(\texttt{R00}, \texttt{R04}, \texttt{R40}, \texttt{R44}) which carry the guide and
wavefront sensors. For each visit and each science raft we select one detector at random,
draw up to 100 stars from that detector, cut a $51 \times 51$ pixel ($10.2\arcsec \times
10.2\arcsec$ at the $0.2\arcsec$ pixel scale) stamp around each star, normalise each stamp
to unit flux, and average them. This yields one stacked PSF image per raft per visit, or up
to 21 per visit. Stars whose stamps would extend past a detector edge are rejected. Across
the $75{,}929$ visits with stamps on disk, the median number of stars contributing to a
stamp is $60$, with a $16$--$84\%$ range of $28$--$100$, and the median number of rafts
represented in a visit is the full $21$.

We stack for two reasons. First, averaging $\sim\!10^2$ flux-normalised stars suppresses
photon noise by an order of magnitude, so that the high-order shapelet coefficients we are
interested in are measured against a much reduced noise floor. Second, a raft-level stamp
is the natural unit for the optical aberrations we are trying to detect: coma and trefoil
vary smoothly across the focal plane on scales much larger than a single detector, whereas
the atmospheric contribution is common to the whole focal plane within a visit. We note two
consequences of this choice that we return to in Section~\ref{results:0}. Stacking
suppresses any aberration that varies \emph{within} a raft, so our scores are a lower bound
on the true per-star asymmetry; and because we draw from a single detector per raft, a
stamp samples one quadrant of the raft's field position rather than the raft mean.

\subsection{Higher-order moment catalog}
\label{data:moments}

For the moment score of Section~\ref{method:moment_score}, and for the comparison in
Section~\ref{results:score_comparison}, we use the adaptive-moment measurements produced by
the LSST Science Pipelines for the same PSF stars. Second moments come from the HSM adaptive
moments \cite{HirataSeljak2003, BernsteinJarvis2002} via the \texttt{slot\_Shape} slot;
third- and fourth-order moments come from the
\texttt{ext\_shapeHSM\_HigherOrderMomentsSource} plugin, which measures the normalised
moments $M_{pq}$ in the coordinate frame whitened by the adaptive second moments, making
them dimensionless \cite{Zhang2023}. The corresponding
\texttt{ext\_shapeHSM\_HigherOrderMomentsPSF} columns give the same quantities for the PSF
model, which we use to form model-minus-star residuals.

From the per-star measurements we form, for each visit, the median of every moment over all
stars in each science raft, and the median over the whole focal plane. This gives a
per-(visit, raft) moment table matched to the stamp sample described above. We also record
the size $T = I_{xx} + I_{yy}$ and the two ellipticity components
$e_1 = (I_{xx} - I_{yy})/T$ and $e_2 = 2 I_{xy}/T$.

\subsection{Wavefront and observing-condition metadata}
\label{data:zernike}

We take three classes of auxiliary metadata from the Consolidated Database (consDB), matched
to our visits by \texttt{visit\_id}.

\textbf{Wavefront Zernikes.} The Rubin AOS estimates the wavefront by curvature sensing on
the four corner wavefront sensors \cite{Xin2015}, which are read out as detectors 191, 195,
199 and 203. From the \texttt{ccdvisit1\_quicklook} table we retrieve the annular Zernike
coefficients $z_4$ through $z_{11}$ in the Noll ordering \cite{Noll1976} --- defocus,
astigmatism, coma, trefoil and spherical --- for each of the four corners. We use both the
median over the four corners and, as a proxy for the aberration gradient across the field,
the peak-to-peak spread $\max_j z_i^{(j)} - \min_j z_i^{(j)}$ over the four corner
positions $j$.

\textbf{Delivered image quality.} We use \texttt{aos\_fwhm}, the AOS estimate of the
delivered FWHM, and \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}, the contribution to the FWHM inferred from
the donut images on the wavefront sensors. Both are independent of our shapelet
measurement and provide the natural comparison baseline in
Section~\ref{results:conditions}.

\textbf{Sky background.} We use \texttt{sky\_bg} from \texttt{ccdvisit1\_quicklook}, taken
as the median across detectors within a visit, as a proxy for the photon noise level in the
stamps.

\section{PSF QUALITY SCORES}
\label{method:0}

In this section we define the two scores. In Section~\ref{method:shapelet} we set out the
shapelet formalism and how we fit it. In Section~\ref{method:atm} we use a simulated
atmospheric PSF population to determine which shapelet modes respond to the atmosphere and
which do not; this is the measurement that motivates the definition. In
Section~\ref{method:shapelet_score} we define the shapelet score, in
Section~\ref{method:moment_score} the moment score, and in Section~\ref{method:tiers} the
quality tiers we use to summarise them.

\subsection{Shapelet decomposition of the PSF}
\label{method:shapelet}

Shapelets are the eigenfunctions of the two-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator, a
complete orthonormal basis in which a localised image can be expanded to a chosen maximum
order \cite{Refregier2003, BernsteinJarvis2002}. We use the polar (Gauss--Laguerre) form
\cite{MasseyRefregier2005}, in which the basis functions carry a definite azimuthal
dependence $e^{im\theta}$ and therefore separate the image into components of explicit
rotational symmetry. This is the property we exploit throughout.

We write the surface brightness of a PSF as

\begin{equation}
I(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{pq} b_{pq}\,
               \psi_{pq}\!\left(\frac{r}{\sigma}, \theta\right),
\label{eq:shapelet}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is an overall scale length, $b_{pq}$ are the shapelet coefficients, and the
basis functions are

\begin{equation}
\psi_{pq}(r, \theta) = \frac{(-1)^q}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sqrt{\frac{q!}{p!}}\;
                       r^{m} e^{im\theta} e^{-r^2/2} L_q^{(m)}(r^2),
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}

with $L_q^{(m)}$ the generalised Laguerre polynomials. The two indices $p$ and $q$ count
quanta of positive and negative angular momentum; we label modes instead by the order
$n = p + q$ and the azimuthal index $m = p - q$, so that $n$ and $m$ always have the same
parity. Requiring $I(r,\theta)$ to be real imposes $b_{pq} = b_{qp}^{*}$, so each mode with
$m > 0$ contributes one real and one imaginary coefficient, while $m = 0$ modes contribute
a single real coefficient.

We truncate at $n \le 6$, which gives $28$ coefficients. Figure~\ref{fig:basis} shows the
resulting basis functions arranged by order. We fit each stacked PSF stamp using
\textsc{GalSim} \cite{Rowe2015}: we first measure the adaptive moments of the stamp with
\texttt{FindAdaptiveMom}, which implements the HSM algorithm \cite{HirataSeljak2003}, and
adopt the resulting $\sigma_{\rm HSM}$ (converted to arcseconds) as the shapelet scale
$\sigma$; we then solve the linear least-squares problem for $b_{pq}$ with
\texttt{Shapelet.fit} in surface-brightness normalisation. Using the adaptive-moment size as
the shapelet scale is what makes the decomposition approximately scale-free: a
well-fit PSF concentrates its power in the low-order modes regardless of how large it is,
so the coefficients measure PSF \emph{shape} rather than PSF size. Throughout, we quote
FWHM as $2.3548\,\sigma_{\rm HSM}$.

\subsection{Which modes respond to the atmosphere?}
\label{method:atm}

The central question for defining the score is which of the 28 coefficients carry
information about the optics and which merely track the atmosphere. We answer it with a
simulation.

We generate $1000$ realisations of an atmospheric PSF. Each realisation is the sum of two
Moffat profiles with independently drawn parameters: FWHM uniform on $[0.5, 1.2]\arcsec$,
Moffat $\beta$ uniform on $[2.5, 4.5]$, and shear components $g_1, g_2$ drawn from a normal
distribution of width $0.03$ and clipped at $|g| < 0.3$. The two-component sum gives the
profile more freedom than a single Moffat, so that the population spans a realistic range of
size, ellipticity, and radial profile shape. Crucially, each component is centred at the
origin, so every realisation is point-symmetric: the population contains atmospheric
variation and, by construction, no optical asymmetry whatsoever. We fit each realisation
with the same procedure used for the data, recovering $1000$ of $1000$ fits with measured
FWHM spanning $0.56$--$1.32\arcsec$.

Figure~\ref{fig:atm} shows the result, and it is unambiguous. The upper panel shows the
standard deviation of each coefficient across the realisations. The even-$m$ coefficients
vary substantially --- the largest are $(0,0)$ with $\sigma = 7.1 \times 10^{-2}$, the
radial mode $(4,0)$ with $\sigma = 2.9 \times 10^{-2}$, and the ellipticity modes $(2,2)$
with $\sigma \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-2}$ --- and together they carry $99.96\%$ of the median
total power. Every odd-$m$ coefficient, by contrast, has $\sigma \le 1.4 \times 10^{-16}$:
they are zero at the level of double-precision round-off. The ratio of the largest even-$m$
to the largest odd-$m$ variation is $5 \times 10^{14}$.

This is not a coincidence of our particular parameter choices, and we emphasise that it is
not an empirical result that could fail for a different atmosphere model. A profile that
satisfies $I(r, \theta) = I(r, \theta + \pi)$ has, by the orthogonality of $e^{im\theta}$,
identically zero projection onto every basis function with odd $m$. Any point-symmetric PSF
--- a Gaussian, a Moffat, a Kolmogorov profile, an elliptical version of any of these, a sum
of such components, and equally an optical PSF from a perfectly aligned telescope --- has no
odd-$m$ power at all. Odd-$m$ power can only be produced by a genuine asymmetry.

The lower panel of Figure~\ref{fig:atm} makes the same point in terms of correlations with
the input parameters. The even-$m$ coefficients correlate with the atmospheric FWHM at up to
$|r| = 0.45$, whereas no odd-$m$ coefficient exceeds $|r| = 0.086$ with FWHM or $|r| = 0.056$
with $|g|$, consistent with noise given $1000$ realisations. We note that the residual
odd-$m$ values are set by floating-point arithmetic in the fit, not by any physical effect.

\subsection{The shapelet score}
\label{method:shapelet_score}

Motivated by Section~\ref{method:atm}, we define the shapelet score as the fraction of the
total shapelet power carried by the odd-$m$ modes:

\begin{equation}
\sshape = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n \geq 3,\; m\ \mathrm{odd}} |b_{nm}|^2}
               {\displaystyle\sum_{nm} |b_{nm}|^2} .
\label{eq:shapelet_score}
\end{equation}

At $n \le 6$ the numerator runs over five modes --- $b_{33}$, $b_{31}$, $b_{55}$, $b_{53}$
and $b_{51}$ --- or ten of the 28 real coefficients once real and imaginary parts are
counted separately. The score is dimensionless, bounded in $[0, 1]$, and invariant under
rotation of the PSF, since rotating by an angle $\phi$ multiplies $b_{nm}$ by $e^{im\phi}$
and leaves $|b_{nm}|^2$ unchanged. Normalising by the total power makes it independent of
the flux normalisation of the stamp, and, because $\sigma$ is set by the adaptive moments,
largely independent of the PSF size --- a claim we test directly on data in
Section~\ref{results:fwhm}.

Two exclusions deserve comment. First, we exclude the $n = 1$ modes, which are odd in $m$
but describe a displacement of the profile rather than a distortion of it. A stamp whose
centroid is offset from the fitted centre acquires $n = 1$ power without being asymmetric,
so including these modes would make the score sensitive to stamp registration. This matters
in practice: in the $i$-band data the $n = 1$ modes carry a median fractional power of
$1.8 \times 10^{-4}$, roughly a quarter of the typical score, so the exclusion is
numerically significant and not merely a matter of principle. Second, we truncate at
$n \le 6$. Higher orders would add further odd-$m$ modes, but at fixed stamp size and noise
level the highest-order coefficients are increasingly poorly constrained, and we find that
$n \le 6$ already captures the asymmetries visible by eye
(Section~\ref{results:gallery}).

Evaluated on the simulated atmospheric population of Section~\ref{method:atm}, the score has
a median of $1.3 \times 10^{-32}$ and a maximum of $1.2 \times 10^{-31}$. In other words,
the atmosphere alone produces a score $29$ orders of magnitude below the smallest threshold
we will use on real data. Any measured score is therefore attributable to the optics, to the
detector, or to noise --- never to seeing.

\subsection{The moment score}
\label{method:moment_score}

We also construct a second, independent metric from the third-order adaptive moments
described in Section~\ref{data:moments}, both as a cross-check on the shapelet score and
because moments are already computed by the pipeline for every star. Following
Ref.~\citenum{Zhang2023}, we form the spin-1 and spin-3 combinations of the normalised
third moments $M_{pq}$,

\begin{align}
c_{11} &= M_{30} + M_{12},  &  c_{12} &= M_{21} + M_{03}, \label{eq:coma}\\
c_{31} &= M_{30} - 3 M_{12}, &  c_{32} &= 3 M_{21} - M_{03}, \label{eq:trefoil}
\end{align}

where $(c_{11}, c_{12})$ transform as a spin-1 object under rotation and describe the
coma-like asymmetry of the PSF, and $(c_{31}, c_{32})$ transform as spin-3 and describe the
trefoil-like asymmetry. The moment score is the weighted sum of their squared moduli,

\begin{equation}
\smom = 3\left(c_{11}^2 + c_{12}^2\right) + \left(c_{31}^2 + c_{32}^2\right),
\label{eq:moment_score}
\end{equation}

which, like the shapelet score, is dimensionless and rotationally invariant. The factor of
three up-weights the coma term; we adopt it for consistency with the metric already in use
for observatory monitoring, and we note that the choice affects the normalisation of
$\smom$ but not its rank ordering strongly, since coma and trefoil are themselves
correlated.

The two scores are built from the same physical information --- the odd-$m$, and hence
asymmetric, content of the PSF --- but by completely different routes: one from a
least-squares basis expansion of the image, the other from weighted pixel moments. Their
agreement, which we quantify in Section~\ref{results:score_comparison}, is therefore a
meaningful validation of both.

\subsection{Score tiers}
\label{method:tiers}

For operational use it is convenient to reduce the score to a small number of quality
classes. We adopt four tiers, with boundaries at $\sshape = 0.002$, $0.005$ and $0.02$:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LOW} ($\sshape < 0.002$): clean, symmetric PSFs.
  \item \textbf{MEDIUM} ($0.002 \le \sshape < 0.005$): mild asymmetry.
  \item \textbf{HIGH} ($0.005 \le \sshape < 0.02$): clearly visible coma or trefoil.
  \item \textbf{VERY HIGH} ($\sshape \ge 0.02$): strongly aberrated PSFs.
\end{itemize}

These boundaries are round numbers chosen to sit near the $80$th, $95$th and $99$th
percentiles of the global distribution; we justify them against the measured percentiles in
Section~\ref{results:distributions} and against visual inspection in
Section~\ref{results:gallery}.

\section{RESULTS}
\label{results:0}

In this section we present the measured scores. In Section~\ref{results:distributions} we
give the score distributions and use them to justify the tier boundaries. In
Section~\ref{results:fwhm} we test the claim that the score is independent of PSF size, and
in Section~\ref{results:gallery} we validate the tiers visually. In
Section~\ref{results:score_comparison} we compare the shapelet and moment scores against
each other. Sections~\ref{results:conditions} and \ref{results:optics} examine the
dependence on observing conditions and on the AOS wavefront estimates, and
Section~\ref{results:monitoring} demonstrates the score as a nightly monitoring tool.

\subsection{Score distributions}
\label{results:distributions}

Figure~\ref{fig:dist} shows the distribution of the shapelet score in each band, for both
epochs, and Table~\ref{tab:percentiles} lists the corresponding percentiles. The
distribution is broad and strongly right-skewed: it peaks near $S_{\rm shapelet} \approx
10^{-4}$ and extends over more than two decades, with a long tail reaching above $10^{-2}$.
This shape is what makes the score useful --- most PSFs are clean, and the metric spends its
dynamic range on the minority that are not.

The distributions are similar in all six bands but shift systematically to higher scores
with wavelength. In DP2 the median rises from $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$ in $u$ to $4.9 \times
10^{-4}$ in $y$, and in post-DP2 from $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$ to $1.1 \times 10^{-3}$. We note
two plausible contributions to this trend, which we do not attempt to separate here: the
redder bands see systematically better seeing, so a fixed amount of optical aberration
represents a larger fraction of the total PSF power, and the $u$ band has the fewest and
faintest stars per stamp, so its scores are the most affected by noise.

Comparing epochs, the post-DP2 distribution is shifted to modestly higher scores than DP2 in
$i$, $z$ and $y$ --- the $i$-band median rises from $4.0 \times 10^{-4}$ to $7.6 \times
10^{-4}$ --- while $u$ improves. We note that post-DP2 covers a period of active work on the
telescope optics, so we caution against reading the epoch difference as a simple
degradation; Section~\ref{results:monitoring} shows that most of the difference is driven by
a limited number of nights rather than by a uniform shift.

The tier boundaries of Section~\ref{method:tiers} were chosen as round numbers near the
$80$th, $95$th and $99$th percentiles, and the measured distributions bear this out. Pooling
all bands, $S_{\rm shapelet} = 0.002$ falls at the $87.1$st percentile in DP2 and the
$79.6$th in post-DP2; $0.005$ falls at the $95.5$th and $93.5$th; and $0.02$ falls at the
$99.5$th and $99.4$th. The resulting tier occupancies are $87.1\%$ LOW, $8.4\%$ MEDIUM,
$4.0\%$ HIGH and $0.5\%$ VERY HIGH for DP2, and $79.6\%$, $13.9\%$, $5.9\%$ and $0.6\%$ for
post-DP2. We note that fixing the boundaries at absolute values rather than at percentiles
is deliberate: a percentile-based threshold would by construction flag the same fraction of
visits every night and could not register a genuine change in image quality.

\subsection{Independence from PSF size}
\label{results:fwhm}

A metric intended to complement the FWHM is only useful if it carries information the FWHM
does not. We test this directly in Figure~\ref{fig:fwhm}, which shows the $i$-band shapelet
score against the measured PSF FWHM for both epochs.

We find no strong relation. The Pearson correlation coefficient between score and FWHM is
$r = +0.152$ in DP2 and $r = +0.110$ in post-DP2, and across all bands and both epochs it
never exceeds $|r| = 0.20$. The running median, shown as the orange line, is close to flat:
in DP2 it varies from $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ at $0.6$--$0.8\arcsec$ to $3.8 \times 10^{-4}$ at
$1.2$--$1.5\arcsec$, a change of only $30\%$ across the full useful seeing range.

The more important point is the vertical extent of the distribution. At every FWHM the score
spans essentially the entire plotted range: averaged over bins between $0.6\arcsec$ and
$1.5\arcsec$, the ratio of the $95$th to the $5$th percentile of the score at fixed FWHM is
$84$ in DP2 and $124$ in post-DP2, whereas the median itself varies by only a factor of
$1.3$ and $3.1$ respectively over the same range. In other words, the scatter at fixed
seeing is one to two orders of magnitude larger than any trend with seeing. Knowing the FWHM
of a visit therefore tells one almost nothing about whether its PSF is aberrated. This is
exactly the complementarity we set out to demonstrate: PSFs in the HIGH and VERY HIGH tiers
are found at all seeing values, including the best.

We do note a real, if weak, effect in the post-DP2 panel, where the median score falls by a
factor of $\sim\!3$ between $0.8\arcsec$ and $1.5\arcsec$ and the rank correlation reaches
$\rho_{\rm s} = -0.22$. We interpret this as dilution rather than as a size dependence of the
aberration: because the score is a \emph{fraction} of total power, a larger atmospheric
contribution increases the denominator and suppresses the score at fixed optical
aberration. We note that this makes the score mildly conservative in poor seeing, and that
the effect is in the opposite direction to any trend that would mimic aberration.

\subsection{Visual validation by tier}
\label{results:gallery}

Any quality metric should be checked against what the images actually look like.
Figure~\ref{fig:gallery} shows four randomly drawn $i$-band raft-stacked PSFs from each
tier, together with the residual after subtracting the fitted shapelet model.

The progression is clear by eye. LOW-tier stamps are round and compact, and their residuals
are consistent with noise. MEDIUM-tier stamps show mild elongation and faint structure in
the residual. HIGH-tier stamps display an unmistakable comatic profile --- a displaced,
asymmetric core with a one-sided flare --- and their residuals show coherent, large-scale
structure rather than noise. VERY HIGH-tier stamps are strongly aberrated, several showing
the double-lobed morphology characteristic of a large defocus or misalignment term. We
emphasise that these examples were drawn at random within each tier rather than selected for
appearance.

The moment scores annotated on each panel track the tiers independently, and their ranges
are almost disjoint: $S_{\rm moment}$ spans $0.0005$--$0.0025$ for the LOW examples,
$0.0064$--$0.0222$ for MEDIUM, $0.0228$--$0.0595$ for HIGH and $0.055$--$0.171$ for VERY
HIGH. We also note that the FWHM values annotated on the panels are interleaved across
tiers, ranging from $0.82\arcsec$ to $1.59\arcsec$ within the HIGH and VERY HIGH tiers
alone, reinforcing the conclusion of Section~\ref{results:fwhm} that PSF size and PSF
asymmetry are close to independent. We quantify the agreement between the two scores next.

\subsection{Shapelet score versus moment score}
\label{results:score_comparison}

The shapelet score and the moment score are constructed from the same physical property of
the PSF but by unrelated numerical routes: one is a fractional power from a linear
least-squares expansion of the image in an orthogonal basis, the other a weighted sum of
squared adaptive moments. Neither uses the other's machinery. Their agreement is therefore
a genuine test rather than a tautology.

We match the two per (visit, raft, band) over the DP2 epoch, for which the per-raft moment
catalog is available, taking the median shapelet score of the PSFs in each raft. This yields
$607{,}948$ matched measurements. Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} shows the result.

We find that the two scores are strongly correlated, with a Spearman rank correlation of
$\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$ over the full matched sample. The correlation is consistent across
bands, ranging only from $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.908$ in $r$ to $+0.936$ in $u$
(Table~\ref{tab:rho}). The relation is close to a power law over three decades,
$\smom \propto \sshape^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 0.95$ and a scatter of $0.24$ dex about the
fit --- that is, very nearly proportional, with $\smom \approx 3.2\,\sshape$. As the right
panel of Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} shows, the median relation is essentially identical
in all six bands, with the curves lying on top of one another. We note that this
band-independence is a useful practical property: a single threshold in either score means
the same thing regardless of filter.

We also computed the correlation using a restricted shapelet score built only from the
$n = 3$ modes, which is the closest shapelet analogue of the third-order moment score. This
raises the agreement slightly, to $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.933$. The improvement is small, which
tells us that the $n = 5$ modes carry information that is largely redundant with the
third-order moments in this dataset. We retain them in the definition because they extend
the dynamic range of the score for the most strongly aberrated PSFs, where third-order
moments alone become a poor description of the profile.

We conclude that the shapelet score and the moment score are close to interchangeable as
rank-orderings of PSF asymmetry. In practice the moment score is cheaper, since the moments
are already computed by the pipeline for every star, while the shapelet score is better
behaved for strongly aberrated PSFs and provides the full coefficient vector for
diagnostics.

\subsection{Dependence on observing conditions}
\label{results:conditions}

We next ask whether the score is driven by anything other than the optics. In
Figure~\ref{fig:conditions} we plot the visit-median score against three quantities
available from consDB for every visit: the sky background, the AOS estimate of the delivered
FWHM (\texttt{aos\_fwhm}), and the donut-blur FWHM (\texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm}).

The dependence on sky background is weak, with $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.12$ in DP2 and $+0.22$ in
post-DP2, and the running median is close to flat across the full range. Some positive
correlation is expected: a brighter sky means noisier stamps, and photon noise projects onto
the odd-$m$ modes as well as the rest. We note that this sets a practical noise floor on the
score for the shallowest visits, and it is one reason we stack up to $100$ stars per stamp.

The two AOS quantities behave very differently from one another, and understanding why is
instructive. The score correlates \emph{positively} with \texttt{aos\_fwhm}
($\rho_{\rm s} = +0.45$ in DP2, $+0.25$ in post-DP2) but \emph{negatively} with
\texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} ($\rho_{\rm s} = -0.33$ and $-0.57$). At first sight the negative
sign is puzzling, since a larger donut blur should indicate a larger wavefront error and
therefore a more aberrated PSF. The explanation is the dilution effect already identified in
Section~\ref{results:fwhm}. We measured the correlation of each of these quantities with the
directly measured PSF FWHM, and find that \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} is very nearly a proxy
for the delivered image size, with $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.81$ in DP2 and $+0.73$ in post-DP2,
whereas \texttt{aos\_fwhm} tracks it much more weakly ($\rho_{\rm s} = +0.37$ and $+0.29$).
Because the shapelet score is a fraction of total power, anything that raises the
atmospheric contribution lowers it at fixed optical aberration. The negative correlation
with \texttt{donut\_blur\_fwhm} is therefore the seeing-dilution trend seen through a
seeing proxy, not evidence that larger wavefront errors produce rounder PSFs. We note that
the positive correlation with \texttt{aos\_fwhm}, which is much less contaminated by seeing,
is in the physically expected direction.

We caution that we have taken these two consDB quantities at face value and have not traced
their precise definitions through the AOS pipeline; the interpretation above rests on the
measured correlations rather than on the definitions.

\subsection{Link to optical aberration}
\label{results:optics}

The strongest test of whether the shapelet score measures optical aberration is to compare
it against an independent optical measurement. The four corner wavefront sensors provide
exactly that: they estimate the Zernike coefficients $z_4$--$z_{11}$ from curvature sensing,
using neither the science pixels nor any part of our analysis. For each visit we form the
peak-to-peak spread of each Zernike coefficient across the four corner positions, which
measures how strongly that aberration varies over the field.

Figure~\ref{fig:zernike} shows the correlation of the visit-median score with each of these
eight field spreads. The result follows the prediction of the mode selection remarkably
closely. The two coma modes, $z_7$ and $z_8$, show the strongest response
($\rho_{\rm s} = +0.170$ and $+0.201$); the trefoil modes $z_9$ and $z_{10}$ follow
($+0.070$ and $+0.101$); and the weakest response of all is the astigmatism mode $z_5$
($+0.028$). Coma and trefoil are the odd-$m$ Zernike aberrations --- $m = 1$ and $m = 3$
respectively --- and they are precisely the aberrations that produce odd-$m$ shapelet power.
Defocus, astigmatism and spherical aberration have even $m$, and produce PSF distortions
that the score is constructed to ignore. Averaging over modes, the odd-$m$ Zernikes give a
mean $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.135$ against $+0.065$ for the even-$m$ ones.

The effect is also visible in absolute terms. Between the lowest and the highest quintile of
field spread, the median shapelet score rises by a factor of $2.35$ for $z_8$ and $2.05$ for
$z_7$, compared with only $1.10$ for $z_5$. Given that the score spans two orders of
magnitude, these are modest but highly significant shifts across $29{,}502$ visits.

We note two limitations. First, the separation between odd-$m$ and even-$m$ Zernikes is not
perfectly clean: the astigmatism mode $z_6$ gives a ratio of $1.42$, comparable to the
trefoil modes, and we do not have an explanation for the asymmetry between $z_5$ and $z_6$.
Second, and more fundamentally, the corner sensors sample the wavefront at only four field
positions, all of them outside the science rafts, whereas the score is measured on the
science rafts themselves. A four-point peak-to-peak spread is a crude proxy for the true
field dependence of an aberration, and we expect this to dilute the correlations
substantially. That a clear, correctly-ordered signal survives this mismatch is what we
regard as the significant result.

\subsection{Nightly observatory monitoring}
\label{results:monitoring}

Finally we demonstrate the score in the role we propose for it: a nightly image-quality
monitor. Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries} shows the visit-median score for all $69{,}053$
(visit, band) combinations over the full $209$-night baseline from 2025 April 24 to 2026 May
13, and Figure~\ref{fig:nightly} reduces the same data to one bar per night, giving the
fraction of raft-stacked PSFs in each quality tier.

The score varies strongly and coherently from night to night. Pooling all bands, the
fraction of PSFs in the HIGH or VERY HIGH tiers has a median over nights of $3.2\%$, but a
$10$th-to-$90$th percentile range of $0.1\%$ to $16.4\%$ and a night-to-night standard
deviation of $7.0$ percentage points. The worst night in the sample, 2026 March 13, has
$28.9\%$ of its PSFs above the HIGH threshold --- a factor of $9.2$ above the median night
--- while several nights, including 2025 May 25 and 2025 September 20, have no PSFs above it
at all. This is a large dynamic range for an image-quality metric, and it is the property
that makes the score useful operationally: a bad night is unmistakable.

The structure is also coherent on longer timescales. Averaging the HIGH-plus-VERY-HIGH
fraction by month, we find $12.6\%$ in 2025 April, at the very start of LSSTCam
commissioning, falling to $0.5\%$ in 2025 October; it then rises again to $9.0$--$9.5\%$
through 2026 January to March before falling to $2.4\%$ by 2026 May. We emphasise that we
make no attempt here to attribute these episodes to specific engineering interventions, and
we have deliberately not cross-referenced the observatory's maintenance logs. What
Figure~\ref{fig:nightly} establishes is that the score responds on the timescales on which
the optical system actually changes, and that it does so with enough contrast to separate
good periods from bad ones by eye.

We note that these figures pool all six bands, so part of the night-to-night variation
reflects which filters were used. We also note that the tier boundaries are absolute rather
than percentile-based, which is what allows a genuine change in image quality to appear as a
change in the plotted fractions.

\section{CONCLUSION}
\label{conclusion:0}

We set out to build a PSF image-quality metric for the Rubin Observatory that is sensitive
to the asymmetric optical aberrations that active-optics control exists to correct, and that
is insensitive to the atmosphere by construction rather than by calibration. We have
presented two such metrics --- the shapelet score and the moment score --- and measured them
for $1{,}468{,}222$ raft-stacked PSFs from $71{,}015$ LSSTCam visits spanning 2025 April to
2026 May.

Our main findings are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Odd-$m$ shapelet modes are exactly blind to the atmosphere.} In a simulation
        of $1000$ point-symmetric atmospheric PSFs spanning realistic ranges of size, radial
        profile and ellipticity, every odd-$m$ shapelet coefficient sits at or below the
        double-precision floor, $\sigma \le 1.4 \times 10^{-16}$, while the even-$m$
        coefficients vary by up to $7.1 \times 10^{-2}$ --- a ratio of $5 \times 10^{14}$.
        This is a consequence of symmetry, not of the particular atmosphere model: any
        profile satisfying $I(r,\theta) = I(r,\theta+\pi)$ has identically zero odd-$m$
        power. The shapelet score, defined as the odd-$m$ fraction of total shapelet power,
        has a median of $1.3 \times 10^{-32}$ on that simulated population.
  \item \textbf{The score is close to independent of PSF size.} The correlation between the
        score and the measured FWHM never exceeds $|r| = 0.20$ in any band or epoch. At
        fixed FWHM the score spans a $5$th-to-$95$th percentile range of $84$--$124$, while
        the median varies by only $1.3$--$3.1$ across the useful seeing range. Knowing the
        seeing of a visit says almost nothing about whether its PSF is aberrated.
  \item \textbf{Two independent metrics agree.} The shapelet score and the third-order
        moment score, which share no machinery, correlate at a Spearman
        $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.923$ over $607{,}948$ matched (visit, raft) measurements, with a
        near-proportional relation $\smom \approx 3.2\,\sshape$ that is the same in all six
        bands.
  \item \textbf{The score tracks real optical aberration.} Compared against the AOS corner
        wavefront sensors --- an entirely independent instrument --- the score responds most
        strongly to the odd-$m$ Zernike aberrations. The coma modes $z_7$ and $z_8$ give
        $\rho_{\rm s} = +0.170$ and $+0.201$ and a factor $2.05$ and $2.35$ rise in median
        score across quintiles of field spread, against $1.10$ for the astigmatism mode
        $z_5$.
  \item \textbf{The score is a practical nightly monitor.} The fraction of PSFs above the
        HIGH threshold has a median over nights of $3.2\%$ but ranges from $0\%$ to $28.9\%$,
        with coherent month-scale structure. A single stacked bar chart, one bar per night,
        separates good optical periods from bad ones by eye.
\end{enumerate}

Several caveats apply to this work. Our PSFs are raft-level stacks of up to $100$ stars
drawn from a single detector per raft, so the scores are insensitive to aberration that
varies within a raft and represent one field position per raft rather than a raft average.
The score is a \emph{fraction} of total power, which makes it mildly conservative in poor
seeing, as discussed in Section~\ref{results:fwhm}; a variant normalised by an external
size estimate would remove this dilution and may be worth exploring. Photon noise projects
onto the odd-$m$ modes and sets a floor on the score for shallow visits, which is visible as
the weak positive correlation with sky background. The tier boundaries are round numbers
justified against measured percentiles and visual inspection, not derived from any
requirement on downstream science. Finally, we have compared against the corner-sensor
Zernikes only through a four-point peak-to-peak spread, which is a crude proxy for the true
field dependence of an aberration and almost certainly dilutes the correlations we report.

Several directions follow naturally from this work. The most important is to quantify the
impact of a shapelet-score cut on weak-lensing systematics directly, through the
$\rho$-statistics and the two-point shear correlation function, which would turn the score
from a monitoring metric into a calibrated selection criterion; we defer this to a companion
analysis. The score can also be computed on the \textsc{Piff} PSF model as well as on the
stars, and the difference between the two would isolate how much of the measured asymmetry
the PSF model actually captures. A variant of the shapelet score has already been adopted
into the LSST Science Pipelines as \texttt{shapeletsIqScore}, and we note that its mode set
differs slightly from the definition adopted here. Finally, applying the score per detector
rather than per raft would allow the field dependence of the aberrations to be mapped
directly, at the cost of a higher noise floor per measurement.
